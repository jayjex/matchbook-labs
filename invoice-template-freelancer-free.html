The six fields and what each does

FieldWhat it does
Invoice numberA unique ID for this bill. It is how both of you refer to the same invoice in email, and how a payment gets matched to the work when money arrives. Sequential numbering (INV-001, INV-002) keeps your own records sortable.
DatesThe issue date starts every due-date calculation, and the due date tells the client when payment is expected without them having to ask. An invoice with one date is a receipt; an invoice with both is a schedule.
ServicesOne line per piece of work. The client uses these lines to approve the bill internally, so a line they can recognize gets approved faster than a line they have to decode.
RatesQuantity or hours times rate per line, shown per row. Itemized rates let a client check the math themselves, which is what turns "please pay this" into "this checks out."
Total dueThe single number the client pays. Subtotal and tax sit above it so the total is auditable, but the total is the only figure that needs an action attached to it.
Payment termsHow and when you get paid: the due date rule (net 15, net 30, due on receipt) and where to send the money. This field is the difference between an invoice and a statement of work delivered.

Three mistakes that slow payment

  • Missing due date. An invoice without one gets scheduled whenever the client's bookkeeping cycle gets to it. "Due in 30 days" and "due when noticed" read the same to a busy accounts payable inbox, and only one of them is your fault.
  • Vague service lines. "Design work - September" makes the client ask what design work, and every question adds a round trip before approval. Name the deliverable per line: "Landing page redesign - 12 hours" answers it before it's asked.
  • No invoice number. Without one, the client's payment reference is "that invoice from Mike", and matching your payment to your records turns into guesswork. Numbering also feeds the paid-vs-outstanding log: every invoice you can count is an invoice you can chase.

Four opinions on filling it in

  1. Invoice on the day work ships, not month-end. (opinion) A same-day invoice lands while the delivery is still fresh in the client's head; a bundled month-end invoice competes with a stack of others and starts its due-date clock on the worst possible day.
  2. Put the total due in the email subject line. (opinion) "Invoice INV-014: $2,140, due Oct 15" gets found in a search for the amount. "Invoice attached" gets found by nobody.
  3. Keep your rate lines even when the client trusts you. (opinion) Itemized lines do their real work six months later, during a tax filing or a dispute over what a project covered. Memory fades; line items do not.
  4. Set the payment terms before the work starts, then repeat them on the invoice. (opinion) The invoice is the worst place to introduce terms for the first time. The five common terms all work; the surprise is what kills collection.

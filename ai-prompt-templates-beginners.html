The anatomy of a prompt: five parts

PartWhat it doesExample line
RoleSets who the tool writes as."You are a support lead at a small online store."
TaskNames the action and its object."Draft a reply to a customer whose order arrived late."
ContextSupplies facts the tool cannot guess."The order was 6 days late; the customer has ordered 4 times this year."
FormatFixes the shape of the output."Three sentences, no greeting, end with a one-line apology offer."
ConstraintSets limits: what to include, avoid, or leave out."Do not promise refunds; keep it under 80 words."

No part is mandatory every time and the order matters less than completeness. A prompt missing context forces the tool to invent the missing facts, and one missing format hands you output in whatever shape it likes that day.

Three worked examples

Each example is a complete prompt you could paste into any AI tool today. Brackets mark the parts you change.

Example 1: an email draft

You are an operations coordinator at a 12-person agency.

Draft a rescheduling email moving a client workshop from [ORIG_DATE] to [NEW_DATE].

Context: the client booked the session for their 30 new hires, and the change comes from our trainer's availability.

Format: a subject line, then 4 sentences maximum. Offer two make-up dates in [MONTH].

Constraint: no blame language, and do not name the trainer.

The role narrows the voice, the context carries the facts a stranger would not know, and the constraint handles the part generic output usually gets wrong.

Example 2: a product description

You are a copywriter for a kitchen-goods brand.

Write a product description for a [PRODUCT], which is [ONE_LINE_OF_WHAT_IT_IS].

Context: buyers already own the basics and want this for [USE_CASE]; the price point is [PRICE].

Format: 2 sentences of description, then 3 bullet points of specs or benefits.

Constraint: no exclamation marks, and do not use the words "revolutionary" or "game-changing".

The format fixes length so you trim nothing afterward, and the constraint list is where brand rules live. Naming the exact words to avoid gives the tool something checkable, which works better than a vague "keep it tasteful".

Example 3: a meeting summary

You are the project coordinator for a weekly 30-minute status meeting.

Summarize the notes I paste below into a recap.

Context: attendees are [TEAM]; decisions matter more than discussion; the meeting date is [DATE].

Format: three sections, Decisions, Owners and deadlines, and Open questions, each as bullet points.

Constraint: keep wording from the notes where possible, and leave out anything marked [OFF_RECORD].

Same five parts as the email example. The one new move is stating the format before pasting the raw notes, so the tool sorts while it reads instead of summarizing first and restructuring after.

Four opinions on writing prompts

These are opinions from writing and reusing prompts across projects, not survey data and not claims about any specific tool.

  1. (Opinion) Be specific about the three things that change output: audience, length, and format. "Make it professional" tells the tool nothing measurable. "Under 100 words for a hiring manager" tells it two things it can check against.
  2. (Opinion) Put your real constraints in the prompt, not in your head. If a vendor name cannot appear, write that down in the prompt. Unstated limits are the ones the output breaks, and fixing a rerun costs more than stating the limit once.
  3. (Opinion) Treat the first output as a draft. Point at the specific part that is wrong and rerun. "Keep the structure, fix the second paragraph" beats starting a fresh prompt from zero, because the parts that worked stay worked.
  4. (Opinion) Keep prompts that worked. Save the exact wording that produced usable output and change only the brackets next time. A personal prompt file beats memory inside a week.

How to pick a palette: the 60-30-10 rule

The 60-30-10 rule is public design knowledge with no single owner; interior decorators, web designers, and presentation coaches all teach some version of it. The split: about 60 percent of the surface gets your dominant color, usually the background. About 30 percent gets a secondary color for panels, cards, headers, and furniture. The last 10 percent gets an accent that appears only where you want the eye to land, like buttons, links, or one wall.

Two habits make the rule work. First, decide the dominant color from the room or screen the palette lives in: a dark app needs a dark 60 percent, a printed flyer needs paper-white. Second, spend the accent on one job per project. When the accent shows up in five places, it stops pointing at anything, which is how busy-looking layouts happen even with good colors.

Mood to palette match (my opinion)

Mood matching is judgment, not measurement, so take this table as one person's opinion shaped by watching palettes get used. Match the feeling you want readers or guests to have first, then take the palette family, then adjust the accent.

Mood you wantPalette family that gets you there
Calm and focusedDesaturated neutrals with one muted accent, like the morning fog family. Low saturation reads as quiet; the single accent keeps focus points findable.
Energetic, a little loudDark base plus one hot accent, like the night market family. Neon on near-black is the loudest combination that still reads as designed rather than messy.
Grounded, handmade, cozyWarm browns with paper cream, like the warm library and field notes families. Earth tones feel worn-in, which is the point.
Premium, editorialNear-black plus cream plus a single rich accent, used sparingly. Premium reads come from restraint: fewer colors, more empty space.
PlayfulTwo or three saturated hues maximum on a light base. More than three and playful tips into chaotic, in my experience.

4 opinions on picking colors

  1. Test the palette in dark mode before you commit to it. Colors picked on a white canvas lie about themselves. A mid-gray that looks elegant on white can vanish against near-black, and a soft pastel accent can glare once the surrounding surface goes dark. My opinion: paste the hex codes into a scratch page and flip the background before writing a line of real CSS.
  2. Choose the accent last, and only after the background is fixed. People shop for a gorgeous accent color first, then discover nothing in the layout supports it. The background is 60 percent of what anyone sees; get that right and the accent is almost any saturated color. Get it wrong and no accent saves the page.
  3. Print it in grayscale once. If two of your five colors come out as the same gray, one of them is doing no work. This matters for anything that will ever be printed: flyers, worksheets, business cards. The print button on this page exists so you can test the cheat sheet itself this way.
  4. Write the hex codes down; do not trust your eyes to match them again. Screen color shifts with brightness settings, and two monitors rarely agree. A palette lives in its codes. Keep the five values in one place and every page, slide, and print job stays consistent, which is most of what people mean when they say a brand looks put together.

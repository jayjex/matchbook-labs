End of Life Planning Checklist (Free): Documents to Locate and the First Five Steps

End of life planning is paperwork organization, and it works better done calmly at a kitchen table than in a hurry. This page is the checklist families ask for most: a locator for the important documents, five first steps that need no lawyer, prompts for a legacy letter, and a short worksheet for memorial wishes. Everything is printable and write-in.

How to use this page: print it, sit down with whoever holds the paperwork, and fill in what you know. Blank rows are normal. Most families start with three or four boxes ticked, and nothing here needs to be finished in one sitting.

Document locator checklist: 15 items

A locator is not the paperwork itself. For each item, check the box when you have found it and write where it lives: a filing cabinet, a fireproof box, an attorney's office, a bank vault. Two rules from the printable binder this page comes from: write only the last four digits of any account number, and record where the password note is rather than the passwords themselves. A sheet that travels between houses should not carry keys, only directions to them.

Practical first steps: five, in order

No step here needs a professional or a formal meeting. The order matters more than the speed: talk first, gather second, decide third.

  1. Talk with your family. One conversation over coffee covers a lot: where the papers are, who should have which role, what people would want to know. It does not have to be a family meeting with an agenda.
  2. Gather what already exists. Walk through the checklist above and find what is on hand. Missing items go on a to-find list instead of a to-worry list.
  3. Fill the gaps one document at a time. A will usually takes one appointment with an attorney or a session with a reputable online service. One document a week is a normal pace, and a half-finished checklist is still useful to the people left holding it.
  4. Pick one storage place and tell one person. A binder in a closet, a fireproof box, or a folder at the attorney's office all work. The list only helps if someone can find it, so name the person and tell them in person.
  5. Put a yearly check on the calendar. Pick a date you already remember, like the week taxes are due or the weekend the smoke alarms get tested. After a move, a sale, or a new grandchild, update the same week.

What a legacy letter includes

A legacy letter is a letter, not a legal document. It carries the things a will cannot: a story, a hope, a recipe, the name of the neighbor with the spare key. Blank pages stall most people, so these five prompts cover what most letters end up saying:

  1. A story from your life you want passed down. The first car, the job that almost wasn't, the trip that went sideways.
  2. What you hope for the people who read it. Plain words beat poetry here.
  3. The practical notes. Where things are, who to call, which bills are on autopay.
  4. A thank-you or an amends, if one is owed. This is the line people come back to.
  5. Something in your own voice. A recipe, a saying, the advice you actually give.

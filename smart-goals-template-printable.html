The five rows

One goal at a time. The prompt under each letter tells you what the row is actually asking; the blank next to it is where the answer goes. If a row stays blank after a minute of trying, that's the row that will kill the goal in week three, so fix it now.

LetterThe promptYour answer
SSpecific What exactly changes? Write it so a stranger could check it. "Run a 10k race in under 65 minutes" clears the bar. "Get fitter" doesn't.
MMeasurable Which number moves, and from what to what? Pick the checkpoint you can count: sessions done, pages written, dollars saved.
AAchievable What makes this realistic with the time, money, and energy you actually have? Name what gets cut to make room for it.
RRelevant Why this goal, and why now? One sentence on what it feeds into. If the answer is "it sounds good", it won't survive a busy month.
TTime-bound The deadline, plus the date of the first working session. A goal with a deadline and no first session is a plan to start someday.

Run the weakest of your three goals through all five rows first. If it passes, the other two probably will too.

Four opinions on goal reviews

  1. The reminder is the review. A review date that lives in your head is a wish. Set the three calendar alerts the same day you fill in this sheet, with ten minutes blocked, not "sometime that week".
  2. Review the trend, not today. Thirty days of data beats one morning's mood. If the log says four sessions out of twelve, work with the four; the number tells you whether to shrink the goal or fix the schedule.
  3. Kill goals on purpose. Ending a goal at the day-60 review means the loop caught a bad bet two months early. Cross it out on the sheet and keep the page: the reason you quit is the raw material for the next goal.
  4. Same chair, same ten minutes. Reviews scheduled as "whenever I get around to it" drift to never. Pick a time and place you're already reliable at (Sunday coffee, Monday lunch) and attach the review to it.

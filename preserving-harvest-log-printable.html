Four record-keeping tips

  1. Label every jar with the date, then copy it here. The label travels with the jar; the log stays on the counter. Matching them the day you fill the shelf is how "was this the dill batch or the garlic one" never happens to you.
  2. One line per batch, same day. A row written while the jars cool is a record. The same row written on Sunday from memory is a story that's drifting already.
  3. Count yield in the unit you'll actually use. If you trade with a neighbor by the pint, log pints. If you cook from jars, log jars. A number you never compare is just handwriting.
  4. Keep the finished sheets with the garden log. Last year's planting dates and this year's batch log together tell you the real story: what produced more than you could put up, and what wasn't worth the water.

What this log is not

It isn't a guide. This sheet has no methods, no times, and no rules on it, and that's on purpose: the method column exists so you can write down what your recipe said. For how to preserve anything, follow your recipe and your local extension office guidance; they know your food and your region. This log keeps the results straight so next season's decisions come from notes instead of memory.

The status legend: what N, C, P, W, and L mean

These five letters are the pipeline stages from the full hub, shrunk to single characters you can write by hand. One row sits in exactly one stage, and the letter changes only when something happens, not when you hope it will.

LetterStageWhat it means
NNewA lead you found but have not contacted. They are on the list because you intend to reach out, and the Next action column carries the date you will.
CContactedA first conversation has happened and they know who you are. Most rows die here, and that is normal. If there is no reply, the row either moves forward after a follow-up or moves to L.
PProposalA scope and a price are in their hands. Every P row should have a Next action with a calendar date, because proposals without a follow-up date quietly expire.
WWonAn agreement exists. The row stops being a prospect and becomes a client: the tracker now records the service, the rate, and the next deliverable instead of a sale.
LLostThey said no, went silent after a proposal, or the budget vanished. Keep the row. Lost rows are the raw material for the win/loss review below, and past clients come back.

A follow-up cadence that fits on four lines

Four habits we would keep if we only kept four. They are opinions from running a table like this, not research findings.

  1. Same day for proposals. The day a quote goes out, write the follow-up date next to it. Seven days is a reasonable first nudge for most work; two weeks for big contracts that route through a decision committee.
  2. Every P row gets a date. A proposal with no follow-up date is a decision you delegated to their inbox. Check the P rows before you check email each morning.
  3. Nudge C rows after two weeks, then park them. Two touches is polite, three is pressure. After a second unanswered nudge, flip the row to L and free the space. A clean L hurts less than a stale C you stopped believing in.
  4. Touch W rows monthly. Won clients are the most likely source of the next project. A one-line check-in a month after delivery keeps the relationship warm without a reason or a pitch.

The win/loss review box

Once a month, answer three questions in writing, for one W row and one L row:

1. Where did the decision actually happen? (First call, proposal, price email, or elsewhere.)

2. What did the client compare you against, and what did they pick or nearly pick?

3. What one sentence would have moved this row faster, and can you say it first next time?

Twelve answers a year, six sentences a month. The material is rows you already wrote, and the questions take ten minutes.

Pipeline math in one paragraph

The reason a client tracker earns its keep is that stages turn a vague year into countable rows. When you log ten prospects, you can watch how many move to Contacted, how many reach Proposal, and how many end in W, and after a few months those counts tell you what a pipeline needs to look like to keep you busy. No template can tell you in advance what your conversion rate will be; it depends on your niche, your pricing, and how warm the leads are. What the tracker does promise is a smaller thing: you will know your own numbers from your own rows instead of guessing. "I need more leads" becomes the more useful sentence "I have 4 proposals out and I usually land about 1 in 3, so I need 3 more proposals to keep the month full," whatever your actual numbers turn out to be.

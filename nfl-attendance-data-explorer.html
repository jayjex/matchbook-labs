NFL Attendance Data Explorer: Game Context by Team and Week as CSV (1999–2026)

Announced attendance figures live in their own datasets. What almost nobody ships is the game-side table that attendance work needs: which team played at home, which week, in which stadium, indoors or out, in what weather, on how much rest. That table is this pack. One flat CSV, 7,548 NFL games from 1999 through the 2026 schedule, keyed by season, week, and team so you can merge an attendance file against it in a couple of lines. Five rows from week 1 of the 2025 season are on this page exactly as they sit in the file.

Straight answer first: the pack has no ticket counts or announced attendance column. It carries the context columns around every game, and the row keys (season, week, team) that an attendance dataset needs to attach to. If you want attendance numbers themselves, pull them from a public source and join them onto this file on team and week. If you want the schedule, venue, and weather side of the same rows, this is it.

What's in the pack

  • games.csv - 7,548 rows, one per game, 1999 through the 2026 schedule, 46 columns. The attendance-adjacent ones: season, week, gameday, weekday, home_team, away_team, stadium, roof, surface, temp, wind, home_rest, away_rest, and game_type. Scores and betting lines ride along on the same rows if you ever want them.
  • team-records-by-season.csv - 861 team-season rows, keyed on season + team. W/L/T and points for and against per club per season.
  • season-summaries.csv - 27 rows, one per season from 1999 through 2025. League-level averages and rates, useful as a baseline.
  • data-dictionary.md - every column defined, including the roof and surface value sets and which years each field starts.

The dataset snapshot is dated 2026-09-06. The 2026 season sits in the file as 272 scheduled games; scores are empty until each one is played. Want the shape first? The free NFL data sample has 100 games with the same 46 columns.

Three attendance analyses this table supports

Attendance trend per team

Join your attendance source to games.csv on season, team, and week, keep the rows where the club was home, and you get the trend plus everything that moves it: a Thursday short week, an outdoor December game, a stretch of road dates that pushed the home slate around. Without the game-side rows, a drop from 72,000 to 64,000 is a bare number; with them, you can see whether week 12 was a by-week for the league or a rain game with both teams losing.

Stadium utilization context

Group games.csv by stadium and season to see how many dates each venue hosted, then split by roof and surface. That tells you what a full home slate looked like for a building before you judge its turnout: a dome club with ten indoor dates and an open-air club with nine weather-exposed ones aren't the same comparison. The columns run back to 1999, so relocations show up as different stadium rows under the same team code.

Week-over-week drop detection

With attendance joined on week, line up consecutive home dates for one club and look at what changed in the context columns: weekday moving from Sunday to Monday, home_rest dropping from seven days to three, temp falling forty degrees. The pack gives you the explanations next to the drop so you can separate schedule and weather effects from anything else. All of it stays at the level of describing data you already have; nothing here predicts or advises.

The join, in six lines

Both sides stay flat CSVs; no database needed. Given a public attendance file with season, team, and week columns:

import pandas as pd
games = pd.read_csv("games.csv")
att = pd.read_csv("attendance.csv")
merged = att.merge(
    games,
    left_on=["season", "team", "week"],
    right_on=["season", "home_team", "week"],
    how="left")

The same season + team keys line up against team-records-by-season.csv when you want win-loss context next to the crowd numbers.

Four tips for the run-through

  1. Baseline with season averages first. Compare against the per-season rows in season-summaries.csv instead of eyeballing raw figures across eras; a 1999 home game and a 2025 one don't sit in the same league.
  2. Filter game_type to REG. The column also holds CON, DIV, WC, and SB. DIV there means the divisional playoff round, not division matchups, so a plain REG filter is the safe default for regular-season comparisons.
  3. Treat empty cells as not tracked. Blank temp on a dome row means the reading doesn't exist, and blank scores on 2026 rows mean the game hasn't been played. Zero-filling either will lie to your averages.
  4. Pin the snapshot date. The file was cut on 2026-09-06. If you re-download after a refresh, note the date in your analysis notebook so two runs from different snapshots don't get compared as one.

This is a data pack for your own analysis. It describes where and when games happened; it contains no attendance projections, no betting advice, and no claims about any team or venue beyond the factual columns in the file.

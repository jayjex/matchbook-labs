How to use the sheet

Print both pages at 100% scale on A4 or US Letter. Fill the core table before you write the character's first scene, then leave it alone: the fields capture a starting position, and a starting position is allowed to be wrong by chapter five. Give every named character their own pair of pages; minor characters can share a single page one with just the name, want, and one relationship row filled.

Page two is the working half. Update the act boxes whenever the arc moves, reread the secret box before any scene where it could surface, and run the scene-presence checklist after drafting anything the character appears in. The five questions take under a minute and they catch the most common failure in a draft: a character who is present in the scene but not doing anything in it.

Four opinions on filling it in

  1. Want and need are different fields, and they should be written in that order. My take: the want is the thing they chase, the need is the thing that actually fixes them, and most drafts break when the two get collapsed into one line. Write the want first as they would say it out loud, then write the need as you would say it about them. Two lines each exists because one line invites a slogan.
  2. Write the wound before the fear. Fear is easy to invent; pick something generic and you will not notice for fifty pages. The wound is the moment that installed it, and once you can point to it, the fear has a history the D-S-B-F check can poke at.
  3. Keep the voice box to four lines. Four lines of sentence rhythm, verbal tics, and one thing they would never say is a usable voice. A page of biography is wallpaper, and wallpaper never survives contact with dialogue. If it does not fit in the box, it probably does not show up on the page either.
  4. Fill the arc tracker in pencil. Act-level plans are guesses, and the guesses change as the middle of the book teaches you what the ending has to cost. Erasing a pencil line costs nothing. Treating your first act plan as a promise is how books stall at 30,000 words.

Print tips

  • Print at 100% scale on A4 or US Letter; page two starts on a fresh sheet automatically.
  • The how-to, the opinions, the print tips, and the questions below stay off the printed page. What prints is the two sheet pages and nothing else.
  • Print one pair per character and keep them together; a cast of eight is sixteen pages in a folder, and that folder is a usable reference in a way no app window is.
  • Prefer typing? Copy the tables into a spreadsheet or a Notion page; the field names transfer as-is.

Questions people ask

Is this character profile template really free?
Yes. Print both pages, copy the layout onto paper, or rebuild the tables in a spreadsheet or a Notion page. No account, no email, nothing to install.
What goes on the two pages?
Page one holds the character core: name, age, want and need with two lines each, fear, wound, a four-line voice and mannerisms box, and four relationship rows with who, tie, and tension. Page two holds the arc tracker with a box per act, the secret the character keeps, and the scene-presence checklist.
What does D-S-B-F mean on the scene checklist?
Desire, secret, backstory, flaw. Four levers a scene can press while it does its plot work. The checklist asks whether the scene moved one of them, showed the want, pressed the fear, and held the voice.
How many characters can I profile?
As many as you print. Full sheets for characters with an arc; a half-filled page one for walk-on parts that only need a name, a want, and one relationship row.

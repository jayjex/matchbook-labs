How to Write an Invoice: The 7 Parts, Field by Field

Most advice on how to write an invoice hands you a form and walks away. This guide walks through the writing instead: the seven parts a written invoice has and what each field is for, four mistakes that stall a bill after you send it, and four opinions on the habits around the writing, labeled as opinions. One sheet per invoice, no accounting system required.

How to use this page: read the seven-part table once, then write your next invoice against it, field by field. The field names in your tool may differ; the seven jobs below show up on every written invoice regardless of what they are called. If you bill from a spreadsheet, the field-by-field template guide covers where each part sits on the page.

The seven parts of a written invoice

PartWhat it is
Invoice numberThe ID for this bill. The client references it when they pay, and you reference it when you chase. Written invoices share it in email threads, bank transfers, and your own records, so one string ties all of them together.
Issue date and due dateThe issue date marks when the bill was written; the due date states when payment is expected. Together they answer the first question every accounts payable desk asks: is this bill mine to pay now or later?
From (who you are)Your name or business name, and how the client can reach you about the bill. A payment that cannot be matched to a sender stalls while someone figures out who billed them.
Bill To (who pays)The client's company and billing contact. In a spreadsheet this is worth keeping as a lookup list: the Invoice Generator Spreadsheet keeps a Clients sheet of 12 slots that feeds the Bill To dropdown, so the same client gets spelled the same way on every invoice.
Line itemsOne row per piece of work, each with quantity and rate. Twelve rows is plenty for a solo worker's bill; the point is that each line names one thing the client already agreed to.
Subtotal and taxThe math between the line items and the bottom line: subtotal is the plain sum of the line amounts, tax is that subtotal times your tax rate. Stacked visibly, these two rows are what let the client check the arithmetic before paying.
Total dueThe one number that gets paid. Everything above it exists so this number survives review without questions.

Four mistakes that stall a written invoice

  • Blank-slate numbering. Hand-invented numbers like "mike-invoice-final2" cannot be sorted, and a client's payment reference pointing at a string like that matches nothing. A sequence plus a zero-padded counter does the job with zero thought.
  • Reused numbers. Two bills wearing one number means the second payment gets applied to the first, and your outstanding count starts lying to you. A sequence that only moves forward is the whole fix.
  • Lumping tax into the total. A single figure with no subtotal above it gives the client nothing to check, and an unchecked number invites a question instead of a payment. Subtotal, tax, total: three rows, stacked.
  • No due date on the page. A bill without one gets scheduled by the client's bookkeeping cycle instead of yours. The due date is the field that turns a bill into something payable on a day.

Four opinions on writing invoices

  1. Number invoices so gaps tell you something. (opinion) INV-004 followed by INV-006 means INV-005 is missing, and a missing number means a bill you wrote and lost. Random or hand-picked numbers hide lost invoices; sequential ones make them visible.
  2. Keep a status label on every invoice. (opinion) Draft, Sent, Paid, Overdue. A bill with no status is a bill you have to remember, and memory is the reason overdue invoices go unchased. The Invoice Generator's log uses exactly these four.
  3. Write the Bill To line the way the client's payer knows them. (opinion) The company name as the accounts payable desk knows it, not the nickname you use in chat. The closer the Bill To line is to their records, the fewer matching questions before payment.
  4. Log the invoice the day you send it. (opinion) One row, written when sending is still in your hand, beats a reconstructed list at month-end. A 20-row log covering paid versus outstanding per invoice is a habit, not a system.

Same README, on the log that backs those habits: the Log is "20 rows of invoice tracking with PAID, OUTSTANDING and TOTAL INVOICED sums", where PAID is a SUMIF over the status column and "OUTSTANDING is the total minus Paid, so Draft, Sent and Overdue count as still owed." The sample file ships "16 sample rows covering 16 invoices: 9 Paid, 3 Sent, 2 Draft, 2 Overdue."

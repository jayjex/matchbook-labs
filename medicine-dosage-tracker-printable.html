Medicine Dosage Tracker Printable (Free): Daily Log Sheet

"Did I already take it?" is a hard question to answer from memory, especially halfway through a busy week. This free printable keeps the answer on paper: one row per dose, with the name, the amount, the time, and a checkbox. Everything on the sheet is a write-in, because the amounts come from your prescription label or the pharmacist, never from a template. This page records what was taken. It does not suggest what to take.

How to use this page: print it, keep it with the pills or on the fridge, and log each dose after you take it: the date, the name exactly as the label spells it, the amount exactly as the label states it, the time, and the checkbox. One sheet holds about ten days for one medication, less for several. When the rows run out, print another. If anything on the label is unclear, that is a question for your pharmacist or doctor; the tracker only records what you did.

The daily log: 10 rows

Ten rows cover a week and a half for one medication. The first row is a gray example showing the format; overwrite it with your own first entry. Copy the name and amount from the label each time, even when you are sure you remember them: labels change, memory drifts, and the log is only as trustworthy as what was copied into it.

Four tips for tracking medicine on paper

Opinions from one household notebook to another:

  1. Keep the tracker in the same place every day. A sheet that moves around stops getting filled in. Tape, magnet, or clipboard: wherever the pills are, the log lives there too.
  2. Set a phone alarm for the fill-in moment, not just the dose. Logging five minutes after each dose is accurate; logging at bedtime from memory is a guessing exercise.
  3. Log the misses, not only the hits. A blank row on Tuesday is information your doctor or pharmacist can work with. A sheet where every row is mysteriously full tells them nothing.
  4. Start a fresh sheet each time one fills up and keep the old ones. Three weeks of filled sheets show a pattern that no single week shows, and patterns are the part worth the bother.

Notes

Four lines for whatever comes up: a question for the pharmacist, a name change on a label, a day the schedule shifted. Questions written down at the moment they occur actually make it to the appointment.

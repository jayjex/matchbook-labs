Leftovers Tracker, Free to Print

A sheet for what is already cooked. This leftovers tracker printable is one page: 10 rows where each container gets a date, a name, a fridge-or-freezer circle, an eat-by date you choose, and a Y/N circle you fill the day the container empties. Under the table sit 3 toss lines for what went out uneaten. Nothing on the page predicts anything; every date and every name is your handwriting.

How to use this page: print it at 100% scale (the site chrome drops out on paper). Fill one row the moment a container goes into storage: the date, what is in it, the fridge or freezer circle, and the eat-by date you want to hold yourself to. Leave the used circle empty until the day the container finishes or leaves, then fill Y or N. The toss lines under the table take what never made it to a row, or what a filled circle has to admit never got used. Reprint when 10 rows run out.

Four opinions on running a leftovers log

  1. (Opinion.) Label the container in the same words as the row. Write the food and the date on the container itself when you write the row. An unlabeled container gets opened, stared at, and closed again; a labeled one ends the negotiation before it starts, and the label keeps the row on the sheet true to the container it describes.
  2. (Opinion.) Put the newest container behind the oldest. The hand grabs from the front, so the front should hold the container that needs finishing first. Stacked any other way, the shelf hides its own history and the log becomes a list of things you keep walking past.
  3. (Opinion.) Fill the used circle the day it happens, not at the end of the week. A circle filled from memory a week later records what you remember, and a row like that stops being worth the paper. One pen stroke on the day keeps the log a log instead of a puzzle.
  4. (Opinion.) Let the toss lines set the cooking, not the guilt. Three lines is a small sample, but it is written evidence. If the tosses keep naming the same dish, the fix is to cook less of that dish or split it into containers the moment it is done. A sheet cannot fix a pattern it never writes down.

What this page is

A waste-organization sheet, and nothing past that. It records what is stored, where, by which date you chose, and whether the container emptied or the bin took it. No storage rule appears anywhere on it, no nutrition guidance, no diet framing, and no guidance on what anyone should keep or discard; those are decisions the sheet never touches. The eat-by column holds the date you write, so if you write nothing, the row still works. What the sheet does do is turn "is there any of that left?" into a glance, and make the toss count visible instead of imagined.

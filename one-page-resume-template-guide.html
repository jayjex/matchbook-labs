One Page Resume Template (Free): When a Resume Fits One Page

A resume does not always have to be one page, but one page is the right default more often than the advice you grew up with says. This free guide splits the call into two short tables - when one page holds, when a second page earns its place - plus a cut list of what to drop first and 4 opinions, each labeled as one. Everything here stays generic on purpose: the page count is a judgment about your history, and the judgment is yours.

How to use this page: find your situation in the first table or the second, treat the row as a starting vote and not a verdict, then run the cut list before you decide the page is full. The cut list usually frees two or three lines you were protecting. The 4 opinions at the end are taste, marked as taste.

The one-page rule is a default, not a law

One page became the default because most careers fit one page, not because a second page offends anyone. A reader skims the top third, checks dates, and looks for one match to the role. Anything that survives that skim had better be evidence. When the evidence is short, the page is short; when a decade of it stacks up, two pages beat one page set in cramped 9-point type.

When one page works

SituationWhy one page holds
School, first jobs, under about 3 years of work(Opinion.) The history is short, so one page holds it with room left. Stretching to two pads the page, and padding reads as padding.
One employer for a long stretch, one path(Opinion.) Promotions inside one place compress well: role, dates, what changed under you. Two pages split a story that reads better whole.
A referred application to a named role(Opinion.) Someone passed your name along, and the reader will look for the one match. A tight page respects the minute they give it.

3 rows, all judgment calls. Your own history gets the final vote.

When two pages earn their place

SituationWhy the second page earns it
10+ years across several employers(Opinion.) The early roles shrink to a line each and the recent ones carry the page. Two pages beat a single page where nothing breathes.
A career change, and both histories matter(Opinion.) The old field shows the pattern you repeat; the new one shows the pivot. Cutting either side hides the story the reader needs.
A field that expects a longer record(Opinion.) Talks, papers, exhibits, and grant lists follow their own norms. Match your field's convention, not a general rule from a blog.

Two pages means two full pages of real material. A page and two lines is a one-page resume with a stray tail; fix the tail instead.

The cut list: drop these first

  • The objective statement. The top third of the page already says what you want and what you bring; the objective restates it in the abstract.
  • "References available upon request." Everyone knows, and nobody calls them before asking you first.
  • Adjective self-descriptions. "Motivated, hard-working team player" claims; verbs and numbers prove. Keep the proof.
  • Every bullet that restates the bullet above it. If two bullets say the same thing, keep the one with the number.

4 opinions, labeled as opinions

  1. Cut every line you can't defend. (Opinion.) If someone asks "what does this line mean?" and the honest answer is a shrug, the line goes. A page of defensible facts beats a page of impressive claims.
  2. Whitespace is a feature. (Opinion.) A page that breathes gets read to the end. Cramming to protect a page count trades a reader's attention for arithmetic.
  3. One page is a size, not a goal. (Opinion.) The goal is a reader who finds a reason to call you inside a minute. If that takes one page, ship one page; if it takes two, fill both with evidence.
  4. Decide the length last. (Opinion.) Cut the bottom first, see what survives, and let the strongest fact move up on its own. Choosing "one page" before the cut is how padding sneaks back in.

This page is a layout and editing guide. Trimming changes what a reader sees first; it does not add experience, and no page count is a job guarantee.

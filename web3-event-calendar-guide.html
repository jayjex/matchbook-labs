Event listings all look the same after the third one: prize numbers, countdown timers, a logo wall. What actually decides whether an event is worth your week is the type of event it is. A build sprint and a community call ask for completely different amounts of time, and showing up to one prepared looks nothing like showing up to the other.

This guide covers the five event types you will run into in any web3 events calendar, what each one is good for, and the short prep list we run before committing to anything. No event names here on purpose; the types hold no matter which calendar you read.

The five event types, and what each is good for

TypeFormatGood forWatch out for
HackathonA build sprint, days to weeks long, solo or in a team, usually around a themeShipping a first version of an idea and meeting other builders in the processSubmission deadlines shift often; the schedule on the event's own page is the only one that counts
ConferenceTalks, booths and side events in one city, one to several daysMeeting people face to face and hearing where a scene is headingTickets plus travel add up fast; decide the budget before deciding to go
Demo dayShort project presentations to a room or a streamSeeing what teams actually shipped, a few minutes at a timeThe schedule runs tight; most of the useful conversation happens around the stage, not on it
WorkshopA guided session on one tool or one taskLearning one thing hands-on, with someone to ask when stuckSeats and materials are often capped, so sign-ups close earlier than the event itself

Formats vary between organizers; treat this table as the common shape, and the event's own page as the truth for any specific date.

Prep mini checklist

Before you commit to an event

  • Calendar block. Put the dates and the time zone into your own calendar first, before anything else on this list.
  • Materials. Charger, notebook, and whatever the event page asks you to bring or install. Packed the day before, not the hour before.
  • Your links. A repo, portfolio or profile URL ready to paste when someone asks what you build. It will come up.
  • Time budget. Decide the hours you can actually give, and write the number down. An event that eats double the planned hours is the one that wrecks the week.
  • Notes line. One page reserved for names, links and next steps, filled in the same day. A blank page at the end of an event means the conversations evaporate.

4 opinion tips for working any event calendar

Opinions, labeled as such. They held up for us; take or leave:

  1. Pick one event per month, not five at once. (Opinion.) Five calendars competing for the same evenings means none of them gets prep and all of them feel like homework. One event a month gets real prep and you show up rested enough to talk to people.
  2. Prep the week before, not the night before. (Opinion.) The checklist above takes an hour when done early and a stressful evening when done late. The whole point of prep is that event time goes to participating, not to setup.
  3. Read the full schedule once before the day. (Opinion.) Pick your sessions while the schedule is still a flat list. Deciding on-site, between two rooms, burns the breaks you needed.
  4. Write three lines the same day. (Opinion.) A name, a link, one next step. Notes reconstructed a week later are guesses with extra steps.

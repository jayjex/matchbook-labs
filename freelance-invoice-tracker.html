Five opinions on getting paid faster

These are opinions from running a table like this one, not survey data and not legal guidance. Take the ones that fit your clients and contracts.

  1. Invoice at milestones, not just at the end. One big invoice after the final delivery means one long wait. Splitting the work into a deposit, a midpoint, and a final invoice keeps money arriving while you are still working, and each smaller invoice gets approved faster than one large one.
  2. Write line items the client's payer can match. "Design work - September" invites a question, and every question adds days before approval. A line item that matches a quote or a scope doc number gets through accounts payable without a follow-up email from you.
  3. Put the due date on the face of the invoice. When the due date only lives in your tracker or in a note under the total, the invoice drifts to the bottom of someone's pile. Top right, plain language, "Due Sep 30". Your tracker still enforces it; the invoice just makes it easy to obey.
  4. Keep a Status column and actually use it. A folder full of sent PDFs cannot be summed. Draft, Sent, Unpaid, Paid on one column turns the whole table into two lists: who to nudge, and what has cleared. Sort by Status and the follow-up list writes itself.
  5. Record the paid date, every time. Paid dates are where your real payment terms come from. After a dozen invoices you can see that Client A pays in 18 days and Client B pays in 41, which is the number that should set deposit sizes and terms on your next contract with each of them.

Invoiced is not collected

The invoice date starts your client's clock, not your bank's. With 30-day terms, cash for work you finished this week can land a month from now, and rent and subscriptions run on the cash calendar, not the invoice one. That gap is why the Status column matters more than the Amount column for week-to-week decisions. The sum of your Unpaid rows is money you have earned but cannot yet spend, and watching it grow while the Paid column stays flat tells you the fix is a payment-terms conversation, not more work.

The three tabs and what each does

However you build it, spreadsheet or paper, three tabs do the work:

  1. Clients. One row per client: name, contact, status (Active, Paused, Lead), default rate, notes. This is the roster you check before saying yes to new work and scan when a quiet month hits. It also keeps history: who paid late, who went quiet, who came back.
  2. Invoices. The table above at scale, one row per invoice with issue date, due date, amount, status, and paid date. This tab is the single source of truth for who owes what, so it is the one you reconcile when a payment lands: find the row, flip the status, fill the date.
  3. Dashboard. No data entry here, formulas only: unpaid count and sum, paid total, income minus expenses, active client count. It answers "how am I doing" without touching raw rows. If a number looks wrong, fix the data in Clients or Invoices, never on the dashboard itself.

Why 66 days?

Honest answer first: no study says a habit takes exactly 66 days, so treat the number as a print decision, not a rule from research.

The famous 21-day figure traces back to Psycho-Cybernetics, a 1960 book by plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz, who noticed his patients took about three weeks to get used to a new appearance. Those were anecdotes about adjusting to surgery, not studies of habit formation. The number stuck because it sounds short.

When researchers tracked real habit formation day by day, the picture got less tidy. A University College London study followed people for 12 weeks and found the road to automatic ranged from a few weeks to most of a year, and it varied by person and by habit. Averages from that line of research land somewhere around the two-month mark, but the spread is the real finding: the same study contained fast learners and slow ones doing the same habit.

So why 66 on this sheet? Two full months and a few days is long enough to outlast the novelty of a new routine, and 66 boxes divide cleanly into 11 across and 6 down, which fits one printed page with room left for the missed-day box. If a habit needs longer than that, print a second sheet or move up to a 90-day grid.

One more useful result from the same UCL study: missing a single day did not wreck anyone's progress. The missed-day box on this sheet exists for exactly that reason, to record the miss and move on.

The three phases you will probably hit

A shape people describe often, offered as common experience, not a research claim. Your dates will land differently, and that is fine.

Days 1–10, novelty

Motivation does the work and the ticks come easy. The usual mistake here is starting five habits instead of three. Fill the habit lines before the motivation fades, and keep each one small enough to do on a bad day.

Days 11–40, the grind

The novelty is gone and the autopilot is not built yet. This stretch is where most tracker pages end up half-filled. When day 23 arrives and you do not feel like it, shrink the habit instead of skipping it: one page of reading counts, one set counts, a walk around the block counts. The cell gets filled either way.

Days 41–66, autopilot-ish

Doing it starts to feel more normal than skipping it, and the grid shows a wall of ticks. The remaining risk is a schedule change: travel, a sick week, guests. Decide the travel version of the habit before you travel, so the streak survives the calendar instead of the calendar killing the streak.

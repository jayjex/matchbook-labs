Host timeline: 8 checks, 2 weeks out to day-of

2 weeks out

  • Pin the date and headcount, then decide your 2 or 3 anchor dishes (the main and a starch are the usual picks)
  • Send the sign-up sheet with categories, not dish requests: mains, sides, dessert, drinks

1 week out

  • Read the sheet top to bottom: every empty row is a dish you now cook, so shop and plan for the gaps
  • Check serves counts against headcount; text anyone who never signed up a single yes-or-no question

Day before

  • Finish your own dishes and clear counter plus fridge space; guests arrive with full hands, not patience
  • Count serving spoons, trivets, and outlets; borrow the gap tonight, not mid-dinner

Day-of

  • Set the table zones from the planner below before anything is hot: oven end, cold end, dessert end
  • Keep the sheet by the door and mark who brought what; it feeds the reminder note next year

4 potluck rules from the host side

  1. Assign categories, not dishes. "Bring a side" gets you someone's best recipe; "bring green bean casserole" gets you a quiet no or a store-bought apology. Categories also make gaps obvious on the sheet while there is still time to fill them.
  2. The host owns the main and one starch. Those two anchor the meal, arrive hot, and give the table its shape. Everything else is negotiable, which is exactly why guests say yes.
  3. Ask for oven time with every sign-up. Note minutes and temperature next to each dish. Two bake-at-400 dishes at once is a plan; four is a line in your kitchen.
  4. The sheet is the menu. Duplicates and gaps are visible on paper two weeks out instead of argued about at the door. Nobody "figures it out" on the day; the sheet already did.

Common questions

How many dishes should I ask guests to bring?

Headcount divided by six, rounded up, minus your own two or three. A 12-person table needs about 4 potluck dishes on top of the main. Fewer, better-made dishes beat a crowded table every time.

What if two guests sign up for the same category?

Let it ride unless the sheet shows three of the same. Two stuffings is a story; three is a problem, and the third guest gets moved to dessert or drinks with a two-line text.

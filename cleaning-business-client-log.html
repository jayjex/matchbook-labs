Page one: the client log, 10 rows

Ten rows, one per standing client. The alias is what you call the client on the schedule, not their full contact card: a name and a street hint is enough to know which door you are walking up to. The address and access notes column carries the details that change the job: side gate, pets, parking, the surfaces to avoid. Frequency is a circle, not a rewrite: weekly, biweekly or monthly, circled once and re-circled when the client changes the arrangement. The rate column holds your agreed rate, written in pen. Row 01 is a filled example; the example rate is a number to overwrite, not a price guide.

Four opinions on keeping a client log

Opinions from building record-keeping pages like this one, not survey data. Take or leave:

  1. (Opinion.) Group clients by neighborhood on the schedule, not by booking order. When the register shows who sits within a few streets of whom, a day fills one area instead of zigzagging across town, and the drive time between stops shrinks on paper before it shrinks on the road.
  2. (Opinion.) Write the rate in pen and leave the column alone until the review you promised. A rate that drifts job to job is not an agreed rate; the log only works if the number you wrote is the number both sides remember.
  3. (Opinion.) Fill next week from this log the evening before, not the morning of. Ten minutes on Sunday turns each row into a day, a slot and a note, and the mornings stop starting with a scroll through your phone.
  4. (Opinion.) Keep the alias on paper and the rest elsewhere. Gate codes, keys and payment details belong in your own locked records, not on a sheet that rides in a van; the access notes column covers what the job needs, nothing more.

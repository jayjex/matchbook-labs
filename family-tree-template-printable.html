Free Family Tree Template (Printable)

A family tree chart you can print and fill in by hand: grandparents on top, parents in the middle, you at the bottom, plus a sibling branch and four lines for great-grandparents if you know the names. Below the chart: eight questions to ask your oldest relatives, six documents to hunt for in the house, and four tips for starting. Print it, write in what you know, and let the empty boxes be the to-do list.

How to use this page: print the chart at 100% scale and start with your own box, then your parents, then grandparents. Fill in names and birth years from memory first. The boxes memory can't fill are the ones to bring to a relative, and the questions and checklist further down are built for that conversation.

The chart: four generations on one page

Work top to bottom or bottom to top, whichever feels less like homework. Most people can fill their own box, both parents, and at least one grandparent without leaving the kitchen table. Everything past that comes from papers and people.

Eight questions to ask your oldest relatives

Records hold dates. People hold stories, and only while they are around to tell them. Call or visit, ask one question at a time, and ask permission before you record the answer on your phone. Each story usually names places and years you can chase on paper later.

  1. What was your first job, and what did it pay?
  2. What was your childhood home like? Who slept where, and who did the cooking?
  3. How did your parents meet?
  4. What did a school day look like for you? Favorite and least favorite part?
  5. What news event from your growing-up years do you remember most, and where were you when you heard?
  6. What did Sundays or holidays look like in your family? Who hosted, what was on the table?
  7. How did you meet the person you married?
  8. What do you wish somebody had asked you about your life?

Write the answers on the back of the chart with the date and the name of who said it. That note is a source, same as a certificate.

Six papers to find in the house first

Before any archive or website, check drawers, shoeboxes, and the back of the closet. These six turn up in most homes, and each one names parents, places, or dates that fill boxes on the chart:

  • Birth certificate (yours, your parents', anyone's)
  • Marriage record or certificate
  • Family photos with names or dates written on the back
  • Death record, obituary clipping, or funeral card
  • Old letters, greeting cards, or an address book with family entries
  • Military discharge papers, union card, or pension paperwork

Four tips for starting

  1. Start with yourself and work backward. Each generation connects to the one you already know, so every box has a witness: you, a relative, or a paper. Working forward from a famous shared surname is how people end up tracing the wrong line for months.
  2. Interview the oldest relatives first. Records wait in archives; people don't. A cousin's memory of who came over on which boat is worth more in week one than any search site.
  3. Write down where every fact came from. A name without a source is a guess, and guesses repeated long enough start looking like facts. One word in the box margin ("Aunt Mei, 9/2026") is enough on the chart; keep the full notes elsewhere.
  4. Follow one line at a time. Pick your mother's mother's line and stay on it until it dead-ends, then switch. Splitting attention across four surnames at once is the fastest way to stall out.

Free archives when the house runs out of papers

Two free resources cover most early searching. The U.S. National Archives (archives.gov) holds census, military, immigration, and land records, open to the public at no charge. FamilySearch (familysearch.org) is a nonprofit-run genealogy site with free record searches and a shared family tree. No affiliation with either; both cost nothing, and both are the usual next stop once the papers at home are used up.

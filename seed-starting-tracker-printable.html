Four record-keeping opinions

Opinions from making seed-organization pages like this one. Take or leave.

  1. Write the sprout date the day you see it, not the day you expected. (Opinion.) The gap between those two numbers is the single most useful thing this table produces, and it only exists if the sprout column holds what actually happened. Expectations belong on your seed packets; observations belong here.
  2. Leave transplant blank until it happens. (Opinion.) Pencil in a plan and the table starts lying: a written-in date looks identical to a happened date three weeks later. A blank column tells you at a glance which seedlings are still inside.
  3. Number your trays and put the number in Notes. (Opinion.) "Tray 2, cells 4–6" in the notes column ties a schedule row to a physical object you can walk over and check. Without the number, a row describes a variety; with it, the row describes a specific tray on a specific shelf.
  4. Fill the labels box before you stick the labels. (Opinion.) Labels get written at sowing speed, upside down, in rain. Copying each label's text into the box takes a minute at the bench and saves the guessing game when a marker fades by June.

What this page is

A seed-and-seedling record sheet. It holds the dates and the notes you write: what went into soil, what came up, what moved outside, what the labels said. It gives no sowing calendar, no germination advice, no zone chart, and no predictions about how anything grows. Every field stays empty until you write your own record in it, which is the same rule the full Garden Log follows.

Print tips

  • Print double-sided for one sheet, or single-sided and park page 2 on the shelf next to the trays.
  • Print at 100% scale on A4 or US Letter. Grayscale is fine; the layout is lines and boxes, no ink-heavy fills.
  • More than ten varieties? Print page 1 again and keep the sheet order stable in the binder; the germination log refers to rows, not dates alone.

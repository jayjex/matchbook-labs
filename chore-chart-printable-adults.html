The weekly rotation: fill the split table in the order you agreed on, and each week every person moves down one row, so the one who had trash all week takes dishes next week and the one on dishes takes whatever is last. Nobody rides the same chore for a month, and the swap is written down instead of remembered differently. Two minutes of rewriting once a week is cheaper than the standing argument over whose turn it is.

Notes

Four tips for chore peace

Opinions from a chart that stuck, not survey data. Take what fits your household.

  1. Specific tasks beat vague zones. "Dishes into the dishwasher, counters wiped" can be checked off. "Keep the kitchen clean" cannot, and an unchecked standard turns every Friday into a debate about what clean means. Write the verb and the object, and the box settles it.
  2. Put a day on every chore. "Trash out Tuesday night" happens. "Trash this week" gets renegotiated every time the bag fills. The Day column exists so the calendar decides, not whoever is home first with the most energy.
  3. Trade whole chores, not halves. If someone hates trash, let them hand it over complete in exchange for a whole chore they mind less. Half-swaps ("I'll do the trash if you do part of my dishes") lose their shape by week two, and the split table has no column for half a chore.
  4. Print a fresh sheet weekly and keep the finished ones. A stack of checked grids answers "I did it twice last month" with paper instead of memory. A blank grid every Monday also resets expectations, which is harder to do on a chart that has been half-faded on the fridge since March.

The sections sit in this order on purpose. The morning checklist is six items because a checklist longer than that turns launch into a project, and the steps are ordinary on purpose - a routine you can run on a bad day is worth more than an impressive one you can only run on a good one. The anchors stay empty for you to fill because a routine copied off somebody else's clock rarely survives contact with yours. The dump box gets six lines because most dumps fit in six, and whatever does not fit keeps until tomorrow. The top-1 line is written at night because evening-you is cheaper to consult than a morning spent deciding.

Four tips for running the routine

Opinions, not science - these come from printing and burying plenty of these sheets, and your mileage will differ.

  1. Pin the sheet where the morning actually happens. Fridge, bathroom mirror, front door - the routine board you can see from where you stand beats the one you have to go find. Visibility does half the work a routine asks of you, so give the sheet a spot before you give it a date.
  2. Same order, forgiving times. The order of the checklist is the part worth repeating; let the clock move around it. Write times in pencil, and after a week the sheet will show you what your real timing looks like - which is usually different from what you planned on Sunday.
  3. The checklist is the floor. If only the six boxes happen, the day still counts. Everything above the floor is a bonus, and a bad day that reaches the floor is a day kept. That framing is the whole reason the launch list stays short.
  4. Reprint instead of salvage. When the sheet on the wall starts getting ignored, print a fresh one rather than decorating the old one. It costs one page and ten seconds, which is a cheaper restart than talking yourself back into a sheet that has gone stale. A skipped week costs nothing here; the template has no dates to fall behind on.

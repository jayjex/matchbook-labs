Fantasy Football Draft Strategy Tips for Beginners

Eight tips for your first fantasy football draft, written the way we'd explain them to a friend running a draft for the first time. Each tip is an opinion from building draft kits and running boards, labeled as ours, with none of the ranking numbers you'll find elsewhere. Below the tips: a write-in planner for deciding when you draft each position, and three mistakes we see every season.

How to use this page: read the eight tips first, then fill in the draft planner table (pen and paper, or print the page and write on it). The planner has no pre-filled answers; what round you take each position is your call, and the table is where you make it before draft night. 8 draft strategy tips (all opinions, ours)

None of these come with numbers attached on purpose. Rankings age badly and change with every league's scoring settings; mechanics like the ones below mostly don't.

1. Group players into tiers instead of one long list. Our take: a single ranked list turns every pick into a crisis the moment someone drafts "your" next name. Tiers work better: cluster the players you'd be happy taking in the same stretch of picks, and when a tier runs out, you slide to the next group calmly. The idea behind it is position scarcity: some positions have a steep drop between tiers and others don't, and noticing where the drops are matters more than memorizing order within one.

2. Watch for runs, and decide in advance whether you join. A run is when several teams draft the same position back to back. Our take: runs are where beginners lose the most ground, because deciding mid-run means reaching for whoever is left. Before draft night, pick the one position you'd join a run for and the ones you'd let pass. Write it in the planner below.

3. Take a backup at every spot. Starters miss weeks; that's the sport. Our take: the most useful bench pick you can make is the backup to a player you already drafted, because you know his situation, his role, and when your starter might miss time. A second starter at a crowded position gives you a decision every week; the right backup gives you a plan.

4. Draft kickers and team defenses near the end. Our take: in our own kits we pencil kickers and defenses into the final rounds. The scoring gap between the options at those spots is small next to the gap at other positions, so picks spent early there are picks spent where they do the least. Wait until the rounds where the room starts grabbing them anyway.

5. Glance at the bye-week column on every pick. Cheat sheets with a bye column exist for a reason. Our take: stacking two or three starters on the same week off is a self-inflicted wound you can only fix by cutting someone you liked. The bye column is a five-second check during the draft that saves a week of headache later; use the blank BYE box on our cheat sheet template to track it.

6. Write one line on why you took each pick. Our take: memory smooths over every mistake, and that's why the same draft goes badly several years running. One short line per pick ("waited too long at this position", "joined the run", "reached for a name I like") turns next year's prep into reading a page instead of guessing.

7. Keep one bench spot for a lottery ticket. A lottery ticket is a player with a clear path to a bigger role if one thing changes: a trade, a depth-chart move, a coach's decision. Our take: the ticket costs you nothing to hold and cuts nothing from your starting lineup. Check on him weekly; when he no longer has a path, cut him without ceremony.

8. Draft from your sheet, not from the room's noise. Draft rooms are loud and everyone has a reaction to every pick. Our take: the whole point of prep is having your answer written down before the moment arrives. When a pick surprises you, look down at your tiers and your planner, not across at the loudest manager.

3 common beginner mistakes

Three we see every season, stated plainly:

  • Drafting on autopilot. Reading a list top to bottom while the room moves around you. The list was written before any pick happened; the room is the part that's live. The tiers-and-planner habit above exists to break this one.
  • Ignoring your league's scoring. Some leagues award a point per catch, some don't; some score individual defenders, some score team defense. The same player changes value between those formats, sometimes a lot. Read the scoring settings before the first pick, not after a confusing week one.
  • Skipping prep entirely. Showing up with whatever list you found that morning. Prep here means three things: your tiers, your filled-in planner table, and the time it takes to read your league's roster and scoring rules. Half an hour covers it.

One honest note: your league's rules beat any guide, including this one. Roster slots, scoring, keeper rules, and pick order differ from league to league, and a tip that works in one room can be actively wrong in the next. Check your league's settings page first.

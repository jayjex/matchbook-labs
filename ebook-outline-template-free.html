Ebook Outline Template (Free): 6-Part Structure + Chapter Template

An ebook outline comes down to six decisions: a working title, a one-sentence promise, an audience line, a chapter list, a one-liner per chapter, and the step the reader takes after the last page. This free guide lays the six out as a fill-in structure, adds a 4-line template for each chapter, and closes with 4 opinions, each labeled as one. It is a craft sheet for planning the writing, not a publishing playbook.

How to use this page: fill the six parts in order, top to bottom, because each one constrains the next. The promise decides which chapters stay. The chapter template then runs once per chapter. Expect to rewrite the title last; working titles are placeholders. Expect to rewrite the title last, after the chapters stop moving.

The outline structure: 6 parts

Each row is one part of the outline. The middle column says what goes in it; the last column is the test that tells you whether the part is done.

PartWhat goes in itTest it passes
Working titleA placeholder name, specific enough to pick the right chapters. A field guide to X beats My ebook at this stage. Final titles get written when the chapters stop moving.You can say it out loud without explaining what the book is about.
PromiseOne sentence: after this book, the reader can X. Written before any chapter exists, because every chapter has to serve it.Every chapter you draft traces back to it in one line. A chapter that cannot gets cut.
Audience lineWho the book is for, and who it is not for. For X who already Y is a working shape. The not-for half is what keeps chapters from drifting toward everyone.You can name one real person it fits, and one request you would turn down.
Chapter listFive to nine chapters, one job each. Numbers here are working titles too. Order exists at this stage but is expected to change.No two chapters share a job. If two do, one absorbs the other or dies.
Chapter one-linersEach chapter compressed to a single sentence: what it covers and in what order. This is the outline's real body; the one-liners are what you reorder.Reading only the one-liners, in order, tells the whole book. A gap in the story shows up here as a missing sentence.
Next stepThe one action the reader takes after the last chapter: a checklist to run, a file to open, a page to print. Decided in the outline so the final chapter can aim at it.It follows from the promise. If you cannot name it, the promise is too vague to outline against.

6 rows. These are planning mechanics, so they hold whether the book ends up 30 pages or 300.

Line 2 is the one that does the work. A chapter promise written as after this chapter the reader can X either completes or it does not; topics that cannot finish the sentence are research notes, not chapters.

4 opinions, labeled as opinions

  1. Outline the last chapter first. (Opinion.) The ending decides what the middle is allowed to do. Write the final chapter's one-liner, then work backwards; chapters that lead nowhere fall out on their own.
  2. One promise per chapter. (Opinion.) A chapter serving two promises serves neither. The one-liner test catches this in seconds: if the sentence needs an and also, split the chapter or cut one half.
  3. Write the one-liners before any chapter prose. (Opinion.) Reordering a table of sentences costs minutes; reordering written chapters costs weeks. The outline is the cheap place to be wrong, so spend the rewrites there.
  4. Decide the next step early. (Opinion.) Books outlined without a last-page action tend to end on a summary. Put the action in row six and the final chapter has a target to land on.

This page is a craft sheet for outlining, not publishing or marketing advice. It does not review drafts, pick niches, or tell you where to sell; those are separate jobs.

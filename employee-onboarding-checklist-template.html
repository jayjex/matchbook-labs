The onboarding checklist, Day 1 to Day 90

25 steps across five phases. Tick the box when done, write the date next to it, and file the sheet when the 90 days close out.

Day 1 (6 items)

  • Desk, keys, and badge ready before the start time
  • Signed offer and ID documents collected and filed
  • Handbook handed over, signed acknowledgment collected the same day
  • Email and tool accounts tested while the employee watches
  • Equipment set up and checked at the workspace
  • First-week schedule shared, paydays and time-off requests explained

Week 1 (6 items)

  • Role expectations meeting with the direct manager
  • Tool training started, access problems logged and fixed the same day
  • Policies walkthrough: hours, leave, and conduct basics
  • Onboarding buddy assigned and introduced
  • First small task assigned and completed
  • End-of-week check-in: questions answered, blockers named

First 30 days (5 items)

  • 30/60/90-day goals written down and agreed
  • Core process documentation read through, questions noted
  • First deliverable completed and reviewed
  • Weekly 1:1s running on a fixed day
  • 30-day review held and written down

By 60 days (4 items)

  • Routine tasks done without step-by-step help
  • Policy and compliance training finished
  • Customer or client exposure arranged
  • 60-day review held, 30-day gaps closed and noted

By 90 days (4 items)

  • Full ownership of the role's core duties
  • 90-day review held, next-quarter goals written
  • Two-way feedback session done
  • All paperwork filed in the personnel record

What the 30-60-90 plan is for

The first month is for learning. Your job as the manager is to get expectations in writing, hand over one small deliverable, and review it fast. A new hire who ends month one knowing what good work looks like is on track. One who ends it still guessing will keep guessing, and the guessing shows up in their output long before it shows up in a review.

Month two is for doing. Routine tasks should now run without you in the room. Close whatever the 30-day review flagged, finish compliance training, and put the person in front of a real customer or client so they see where their work actually lands. This is also the window where quiet problems (unclear priorities, a tool they never learned) either get fixed or calcify.

Month three is for ownership. Core duties run end to end, next-quarter goals get written down, and both sides say out loud whether the fit works. The 90-day review is where onboarding either ends or gets extended; without that conversation, a rough start just quietly becomes the permanent arrangement.

Write it down at every phase. A dated checklist in a file answers "was that ever done"; a memory of a hallway chat answers nothing. The form matters less than the names and dates on it, which is what makes the reviews at 30, 60, and 90 days take an hour instead of an afternoon.

Paper or digital onboarding?

Paper first, digital later. A printed checklist on the manager's desk gets ticked; a form buried in a tab list gets forgotten, and at small scale the printout also becomes the personnel record copy you were going to file anyway. Move to digital once you have remote hires or more than a handful of employees, because a spreadsheet can be shared and a paper sheet cannot. Both fail the same way when nobody owns the list, so name one owner and check progress weekly whichever format you pick.

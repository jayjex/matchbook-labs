LMSW Study Plan: 8 Weeks, Mapped Out

A workable study plan for the LMSW fits in eight weeks at eight to ten hours a week. This page lays out those eight weeks: what to study, how many hours to give it, which practice sets to use, and the five mistakes that waste the most time.

Who this plan assumes you are

You have a master's degree in social work or are close to finishing one, and your exam date sits about eight weeks out. If you are further from your coursework or working a heavy caseload, keep the weekly structure and stretch the calendar to ten or twelve weeks instead of stacking more hours onto the same eight. The order of the weeks matters more than the exact dates.

Five study mistakes that waste weeks

1. Rereading notes instead of answering questions

Rereading builds recognition, and the exam does not ask for recognition. It hands you a vignette and four defensible options and asks you to commit. The fix is the loop: answer questions, check answers, read rationales, repeat. That loop is the whole job, and notes are a reference for it, not a substitute.

2. Saving timing practice for the end

Pacing is a separate skill from content, and it needs its own rehearsal. Time yourself from week 4 onward so the clock is boring by exam day. Candidates who know the material still run long when the first time they face 170 questions against a clock is the exam itself.

3. Treating ethics as one chapter

Ethics-style judgment shows up inside development, assessment, and intervention vignettes too, which is why our opinion is that quarantining it into a single week is a mistake. Use the ethics week to go deep, but also drop one ethics question into every practice set you take from week 2 on. Isolated ethics practice trains isolated ethics skills.

4. Reviewing only the questions you missed

A guess you got right is a gap you have not found yet. Mark every question you were unsure about during a set, and read those rationales with the same attention you give your misses. Otherwise the same lucky coin-flip keeps deciding your score.

5. Taking the mock exam in comfortable sittings

Splitting 170 questions across three evenings trains the wrong stamina. Sit each full mock in one timed block, under the same conditions you expect on exam day. Endurance is part of what you are practicing, and it is cheaper to discover a stamina problem in week 7 than on test day.

Pacing the exam: the 1.3-minute math

The exam is a 170-question multiple-choice test, and the appointment typically runs about four hours. That is the typical shape people describe, so treat these numbers as planning figures and confirm the exact appointment length in your regulator's handbook.

170 questions. Appointment: roughly 240 minutes. Instructions, answer-sheet setup, and a short break eat about 25 to 30 minutes, leaving 210 to 215 minutes of question time.

210 ÷ 170 = 1.24 minutes per question. 215 ÷ 170 = 1.26.

Plan on 1.3 minutes per question (78 seconds). That is slightly more than the arithmetic requires, and the slack absorbs the vignettes that run long.

Three habits make that pace hold:

  • Checkpoints. At question 60 you should be around 78 minutes in (60 × 1.3). At question 120, about two hours 36 minutes. If you are more than 10 minutes behind either mark, start triaging: answer, flag, move on, return only if time remains.
  • No blank rows. Wrong answers typically carry no penalty on this exam type, so an empty row is the only guaranteed zero. Verify the scoring policy in your handbook, then answer every row.
  • Long vignettes. Some stems run well past 100 words. For those, read the actual question line first, then the vignette, then the options. It saves a full re-read.

Rest and recovery

  1. One full day off per week, scheduled. Treat the day off as part of the plan, not a failure of discipline. Week 6 should feel no heavier than week 2, and a fixed recovery day is what makes that possible.
  2. Protect sleep in the final 48 hours. Sleep is when the material you studied turns into recallable memory. An all-nighter swaps your best consolidation window for two foggy hours of extra review, and the trade is bad.
  3. Nothing new in the last two days. After the second mock, review only the one-line rules you wrote for yourself across the previous weeks. Then stop. The last job of the plan is to arrive rested.

Frequently asked

How many hours a week should I study for the LMSW exam? Eight to ten focused hours a week is a workable range if you are a year or two out of your master's program, which is what this plan assumes. Further from school, or working a heavy caseload, keep the weekly hours and stretch the plan to ten or twelve weeks instead of adding nights.

Is 8 weeks enough to study for the LMSW exam? Usually, with eight to ten focused hours a week and two full timed mocks completed before exam week. It is a tight but real window for recent graduates. If your week-1 diagnostic comes back weak across every domain, stretch to twelve weeks; the weekly structure stays the same.

What should I do the day before the exam? Logistics first: confirm the appointment time, route, and required ID against your regulator's handbook. Then one light pass over your rule notes, a normal meal, and sleep. Cramming new content the night before costs more in sleep than it returns in recall.

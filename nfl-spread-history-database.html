One CSV with every NFL game from 1999 through the 2026 schedule, each row carrying the closing point spread, total, and moneyline recorded for that game. What's in the pack

  • games.csv - 7,548 rows, one per game, 1999 through the 2026 schedule, 46 columns. Final scores, closing spread (spread_line), closing total (total_line), moneylines, spread and total odds, rest days, starting QBs, coaches, referee, stadium, roof, surface, temperature, and wind on every game where the value was tracked.
  • team-records-by-season.csv - 861 team-season rows. W/L/T, points for and against, ATS wins/losses/pushes, ATS win percentage, and over/under records, straight back to 1999.
  • season-summaries.csv - 27 rows, one per season from 1999 through 2025. League-level averages, home win percentage, favorite and underdog cover rates, and over/under splits.
  • data-dictionary.md - every column defined, including which years each field starts.

Three ways people use it

Hobby analysis

Whether dome teams score differently outdoors, how home-field advantage moved after 2020, how often favorites covered in a given era. Questions like these need scores, spreads, and stadium context in one place, and scraping them out of box scores is its own weekend project. Here it's one file and a pivot table.

Backtesting a rule you already have

If you keep a rule of thumb about road divisional dogs or prime-time unders, the honest test is replaying it against closing lines for every game it would have applied to. Twenty-seven seasons of games is enough to see whether a pattern survives contact with the data. Often it doesn't, and that's a useful answer too.

Fantasy and matchup research

QB names, coaches, rest days, weather, roof, and surface are columns on every game row, next to the betting numbers. When you're pricing a matchup or arguing about a scoring environment, the context is in the same file as the results.

This is clean data for your own analysis. It records what happened and where lines closed; it doesn't tell anyone what to bet, and nothing here should be read as betting advice.

Coverage, honestly

  • One line per game, not a movement log. The source records a single closing snapshot of each spread and total, taken when the dataset was built (2026-09-06). You get where the line settled, which is the number most analysis runs against. If your model needs the opening number or intraweek movement, you'll need a second source.
  • Which fields start when. Spreads and totals run 1999 onward and sit on 7,388 of 7,548 rows. Moneylines and the American odds fields start in 2006. Some 1990s rows are missing the field entirely rather than showing a zero.
  • The 2026 season is scheduled, not played. The 272 rows for this season have games and, where posted, lines; scores are empty until each game is played.
  • Franchise history stays put. Relocated teams keep their old codes (OAK, SD, STL), and Washington appears as WAS through 2021 and WSH from 2022. The dictionary spells all of this out.

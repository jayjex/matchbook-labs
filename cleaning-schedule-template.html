The weekly rota, Monday to Sunday

Six lines per day, enough for a solo cleaner's real week. Write the client and the job on each line, then mark it R for recurring (a standing client) or O for one-off. The recurring lines mostly stay put from week to week; the one-off lines are where the rota gets edited. Pencil first, ink after the client confirms. The rota and its header fit one printed page.

Travel buffer, the line item nobody quotes

Drive time between jobs eats margin, and a fresh schedule always forgets it. Five jobs at two hours each reads like a ten-hour day until you add the road: four gaps at 25 minutes is another hour and 40 minutes, unpaid, before fuel. That gap math is arithmetic, not a rule, but it is why the arrival window above should come from last week's actuals rather than from hope. Write the windows wide, let the rota carry the travel on paper, and the day stops leaking into your evening.

Four opinions on route density

These are opinions from working the loop, not survey data. Take the ones that fit your map and your agreements.

  1. Schedule by neighborhood, not by booking order. Two clients ten minutes apart, back to back, beat one across town even when the booking order looked tidy. The client-day map makes the clusters visible; the rota is where you act on them.
  2. Leave Friday slack. Friday absorbs the reschedule, the deep clean that ran over, and the same-day call. A full Friday is a week with nowhere to put the job that moves.
  3. Send the farthest job first. In morning traffic the long leg costs less at 7 am than at 10 am, and starting far leaves the afternoon for the cluster near home.
  4. Cap heavy days at one. A deep clean or a move-out anchors a day. Two on the same crew, same day, is how the second one runs long and the callback happens.

Deep-clean rotation, room of the week

Snowball vs avalanche, with real numbers

Snowball pays the smallest balance first. Avalanche pays the highest APR first. Avalanche is the cheaper order on paper; snowball hands you a finished debt sooner. Here is the same $500 a month spent both ways, interest charged monthly (APR ÷ 12) on the running balance, every spare dollar rolling to the first debt in the chosen order:

OrderFirst debt deadAll debts deadTotal interest paid
Snowball (store card first)month 10month 21about $1,803
Avalanche (credit card first)month 13month 21about $1,503

The two debts in the example:

  • Store card: $2,900 balance, 18.99% APR, $75 minimum
  • Credit card: $4,600 balance, 27.99% APR, $167 minimum

Minimums total $242, so $258 rolls to the target debt every month on a $500 budget. Both orders finish in month 21, because the budget is the budget. The gap is $300 of interest and the shape of the win: the snowball kills the $2,900 card at month 10, the avalanche kills the $4,600 card at month 13 and pays $300 less for the privilege. With a $258 extra payment and these two APRs, that is the whole gap. Widen the APR spread or shrink the extra payment and the avalanche's edge grows; the snowball's edge (an early win you can color in) does not.

Where the cash envelope system fits

The snowball tells the extra dollars where to go. Envelopes make sure the extra dollars exist. If you have not set the envelopes up yet, the free cash envelope template is one printable pocket with the transaction log on the back. Once minimums and the extra payment are committed, the rest of the month runs on cash in labeled envelopes: groceries, gas, dining out, fun money. When an envelope is empty, that category waits until next month, or you make a trade from another envelope and write it down. That feedback loop is the cash envelope system, and it is what stops new balances from growing while you pay the old ones down. The full pack prints 14 of them: cut on the solid border, fold on the dashed line, log every move on the back.

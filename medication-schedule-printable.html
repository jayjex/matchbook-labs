Medication Schedule Printable (Free): Weekly Chart, Pharmacy Box, and Refill Tracker

Most medication mix-ups are paper problems: the schedule lives in one person's head, the refill dates live on receipts, and the pharmacy number lives in somebody's phone. This free printable puts all three on one sheet. You fill in the med names yourself from the pharmacy label, then tick circles through the week. There are no meds, doses, or health claims printed here; it is an organizer for whatever the label and the doctor already said.

How to use this page: print it, copy each med name and the dose exactly as written on the pharmacy label, and post the sheet where the pills live. Circle or tick the day's dot after each slot is handled. When a doctor or pharmacist changes anything, cross out the old line, write the new one, and put the date next to it. For anything about what a medication is or how much to take, the label and the prescriber's office are the source; this chart only tracks what was done.

Weekly medication chart: 7 days, 5 time slots

Five slots cover a full day: morning, midday, evening, bedtime, and as-needed. Each slot has two write-in columns (med name, dose) and seven circles, one per day. Fill the med column from the label, not from memory. If a slot is empty on some days, leave the circles blank; an unfilled slot says something too.

Four tips for a medication routine that holds

Opinions from one household notebook to another:

  1. Use a weekly pill box and fill it once a week. Same day every week, say Sunday evening. The box answers "did I already take it?" at a glance, which is the question the chart circles double-check.
  2. Set phone alarms named for the slot, not the med. "Morning meds" survives a schedule change; "blue pill at 8" does not. One alarm per slot beats one per med.
  3. Keep one med list for every doctor. Every office asks for the same thing. One current list, shown at each visit, is how the professionals catch what a family would miss. Hand them the list; let them do their job with it.
  4. Anchor the routine to something that already happens. Breakfast, the 7am coffee, teeth brushing. Habits stick to other habits; a free-floating "sometime after lunch" does not survive a busy week.

Questions for the doctor

Questions arrive at strange hours and leave before the appointment. Keep the box on the same clipboard as the chart and write them down as they come. Four lines, one question each; the front of the line goes to whichever question has been waiting longest.

Page one: the supply inventory list, 12 rows

Twelve rows, one per supply. Write the item in plain words you will recognize in a store aisle, not a product name: "glass spray," "microfiber cloths," "trash liners, tall kitchen." The have column is a circle, not a count: full, half, or out, circled on sight so the walk-through takes minutes. The need column circles how urgent the gap is: today, or this week. The quantity column holds the number you actually counted, and it is the column that pays for the whole page, because a written quantity stops the "we still had those" purchases. Row 01 is a filled example; overwrite it with your own supplies and counts.

Four opinions on keeping a cleaning supply inventory list

Opinions from building record-keeping pages like this one, not survey data. Take or leave:

  1. (Opinion.) Keep one caddy per floor and let the map say so. A business that hauls everything up and down stairs all day loses the time the inventory was supposed to save; a caddy per floor, each with its own row on the storage map, ends the hauling and makes the count a two-minute sweep instead of a housewide hunt.
  2. (Opinion.) Write the reorder level next to the item name, not in your head. "Restock cloths when fewer than five are clean" is a rule you can hand to anyone; "I'll notice" is a rule only you can follow, and you will notice at the worst job of the week instead.
  3. (Opinion.) Pick one restock day and let everything wait for it. Buying against one weekday turns twelve small errands into one stop, and the need circles from page one tell you whether a run is worth breaking the rule: today only wins when the circle says today.
  4. (Opinion.) Count by location, not by memory. Walking the five storage spots in the order the map lists them takes one pass and finds the duplicates; remembering what you bought last week finds nothing, because the bottle you forgot lives in the car bin you never opened.

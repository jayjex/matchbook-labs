Season notes

Frost dates are local. Take your first and last frost dates from local sources: an extension office near you, an almanac for your region, or a gardening neighbor who has kept notes longer than you. Write them in pencil and adjust as your own records pile up. The two spare lines take whatever else the season teaches you, like when the ground finally dried out or which bed stayed soggy. Harvest tally

Six lines for the first picks of each crop. The planting table says what went in; the tally says what came out. Amount can be a count, a weight, or a phrase like "two colanders full," whichever unit you will actually repeat.

Four record-keeping tips

  1. Log the same day. A row written at the end of planting day is a fact. The same row written three days later is a guess with confidence.
  2. Take days-to-harvest from the packet. Write the number printed on the packet, even if a neighbor swears theirs runs faster. The log is a record of what the packet promised against what your beds did, and that comparison is the useful part.
  3. Frost dates in pencil, always. Your own notes, kept over a couple of seasons, describe your yard better than any printed table can. Redraw the dates when your records disagree with them.
  4. Keep the finished sheets. Last year's log is next year's plan already written: which bed held tomatoes, how long the beans actually took, which packet had too many seeds. A drawer of used sheets beats a fresh memory every spring.

Seed inventory

Six lines so the seed box stops surprising you. Write the variety and when you bought it, then update the quantity left as packets get used up. Next season's order gets written against this table instead of memory, which is how duplicate zucchini packets happen.

Bought can be a year or a full date; whatever you'll remember checking. Qty left can be "half packet" if counting seeds feels silly. The table only needs to be readable by the person holding the wallet at the seed order.

Print tips

  • One sheet per season covers most gardens. Print two if you run a lot of beds, one per half of the garden.
  • Print at 100% scale on A4 or US Letter. Grayscale is fine; the layout is lines and boxes, no ink-heavy fills.
  • Hole-punch straight into the garden binder. The tables are full-width with standard borders, so they punch cleanly on both paper sizes.
  • Prefer typing? Copy the tables into a spreadsheet; the columns transfer as-is.

Interview Schedule Template (Free)

An interview schedule template turns "wait, who is meeting whom at what time?" into a sheet on the wall. These two printable pages hold one hiring round. Page 1 is the schedule: eight rows, each with the time, the candidate's name, the role, and the interviewer, all write-in. Page 2 travels with it: five lines for the questions every interviewer asks, and a four-row scorecard where each candidate ends up with a circled 1 to 5. As a candidate interview tracker printable, free to reprint whenever the next round starts. The full HR Ops Kit is linked at the bottom.

How to use this page: print at 100% scale, two sheets. Page 1 fills in as interviews get booked: one row each, time first, then candidate, role, and who is conducting it. Tape it where the team walks past, so nobody has to text "when is the 2 o'clock?" Page 2 goes to each interviewer the morning of: the questions box holds the five questions you decided on, and after each interview the interviewer circles 1 to 5 on the candidate's row before the next one walks in. Eight rows full and more candidates coming? Print another sheet.

The two-page interview sheet

Everything on the sheet is write-in on purpose: no preset times, no preset questions, no roles filled in. The rows stay blank until an interview exists. The role column repeats when one round covers several openings, and stays blank per row when the interviewer should ask the candidate which seat they applied for. Page 2's questions box is five lines because that fits on one sheet with the scorecard; whatever does not fit in five lines is not going to get asked consistently anyway. The scorecard rows hold up to four candidates per sheet, one circled score each, written by whoever interviewed them.

The sheet organizes the day: who meets whom, when, and what got circled. It does not make the decision; the circled numbers are the interviewers' input, and comparing them across rows is how the hiring conversation starts, not how it ends. A score with no memory behind it is a mood with a number on it, which is why the circle goes in right after the interview.

4 opinions on running an interview day

  1. Same questions every candidate. (Opinion.) Decide the five in the questions box before the first interview and ask them in the same order each time. It is the only way the scorecard rows mean the same thing by the end of the day: candidate 1 answered question 3, and so did candidate 4. Making questions up per candidate turns the scorecard into four unrelated conversations.
  2. Leave a gap between rows on page 1. (Opinion.) Ten minutes, minimum, between one interview's end time and the next one's start. The gap is where the interviewer writes the score and the sentence they will want to remember tomorrow. Booked back to back, the circle gets filled from a first impression that is already fading into the next handshake.
  3. Circle the score before the next interview starts. (Opinion.) The scorecard row for candidate 2 should not be blank when candidate 3 walks in. Scores drift toward the average of what happened since, and by end of day nobody can tell a 3 from a 4 anymore. The gap from tip 2 exists to make this one possible.
  4. Send page 2 to the interviewers the day before. (Opinion.) The questions box only works if every interviewer read it before the candidate sits down. Handing someone the sheet in the hallway two minutes before is how you get five questions asked five different ways. One print per interviewer, the questions already filled in by the hiring lead.

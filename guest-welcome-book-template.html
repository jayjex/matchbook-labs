Four opinions on welcome books

These are opinions from filling in and reprinting sheets like this one, not survey data. Take what fits your unit.

  1. Laminate one copy and write on it with a wet-erase marker. Codes and checkout times change; the laminate lets you wipe and rewrite in a minute instead of reprinting the book every stay. Keep the paper master in your binder so the next laminate starts from a clean template.
  2. Put it where guests land, not where it photographs well. The book works if it is under the wifi box on the entry table or leaning against the coffee tin. Filed upright on a shelf in the hallway, it is decoration. Guests read what is already in their hand when they wonder about the door code.
  3. Two pages, then stop. Every page you add lowers the share of the book that gets read. A short book with big type and a few blank lines beats a booklet with a paragraph about the neighborhood's history. If something does not change a guest's first hour or checkout, it can live in your host notes instead.
  4. Add a line every time a guest asks. Questions in your inbox are the gaps in the book. The third time someone asks where the corkscrew lives or which switch runs the porch light, write it in that night. A welcome book that grows from real questions stays useful; one written in a single sitting guesses.

What guests actually read

Honest version: most guests skim this book once, photograph the wifi line, and never open it again. Three sections carry almost all the traffic: the wifi and door code box, the checkout steps, and any rule with money attached, like parking or pets. Guests read those because those three decide whether their stay starts smoothly and whether their deposit survives. Write and check them first, keep them on page 1, and treat the rest of the book as reference that earns its place only when it stops a message from being sent. A longer welcome book is not a warmer welcome; it is a slower search for the three lines people came for.

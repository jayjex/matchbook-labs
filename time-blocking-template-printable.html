The numbers on the sheets are choices, not findings. Twelve rows because a day rarely holds more slots worth naming; a longer list turns the grid into a wish list. The time column is empty on purpose - the sheet doesn't decide whether your blocks run 30 minutes or 3 hours, and one filled week will teach you more about your real slot lengths than any guess. Three block columns per day because a week page that looks fully booked stops being read by Wednesday, and blank cells show evenings and days off for what they are. The interruption log sits on the week sheet so one glance on Friday covers five days, not five sheets.

4 opinions on running a time blocking sheet

Opinions, not science - these come from printing and burying plenty of these pages, and your mileage will differ.

  1. One task per slot. (Opinion.) "Admin work" fills a row and finishes nothing; "draft the invoice email" either fits the slot or it doesn't. If a task needs two slots, write it on two rows and let the done circles show the halfway point.
  2. Set your slot lengths after the first week, not before. (Opinion.) The empty time column is deliberate: most people guess their lengths optimistically, then spend a week redoing the grid. Yesterday's filled day grid is the only honest source for how long your work takes.
  3. Leave the week page partly blank on purpose. (Opinion.) Three columns per day is a ceiling, not a quota. Blocks that fit around school runs, standups, or a slow Friday survive contact with the week; a fully inked week page gets abandoned the first time life shows up.
  4. Write interruptions down instead of chasing them. (Opinion.) The log asks for two things: when it happened and what it was. Writing "14:00, landlord called" costs less than arguing with the interruption, and four logged lines tell you more about what eats a schedule than a memory of a bad Tuesday.

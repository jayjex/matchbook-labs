Treats and Rewards, Explained

Food works for training because it buys you many repetitions fast: a puppy will work for a treat five times in two minutes, where a game of fetch costs more time than it returns. Three things make the treats effective:

  • Size. Soft pieces about the size of a pea. The goal is a puppy still hungry for rep number ten, not one full after number two.
  • Timing. The treat lands within a second of the behavior you want. A late reward pays for whatever the puppy did next, which is often the opposite of what you asked.
  • Fading. Once a skill is marked R for several days, stop paying every rep. Praise and the occasional treat keep it alive; permanent treats make the skill negotiable.

If the puppy loses interest in food, that is data for the log: full, tired, or stressed are the usual causes, and all three mean stop and try again later. Count training pieces as part of the day's food, and for food choices and portion sizes, ask your vet. Pair the log with the puppy weekly routine chart so sessions land in the same blocks every day.

4 Habits That Keep Training Consistent

  1. Two or three minutes, several times a day. A 20-minute Sunday marathon teaches the puppy to be tired of you, not to sit. Short sessions end while the puppy still wants more, and that wanting is what brings it back tomorrow.
  2. Same cue words from the whole house. "Sit," "sit down," and "butt on the floor" are three different words to a puppy. Pick one per skill, write it at the top of the log sheet, and hold everyone to it.
  3. Fill the log the same day, even badly. A scribbled note at 7pm ("4 of 5 reps, no lure on last one") beats the tidy update you never get to. The sheet on the fridge makes this the easy default.
  4. End on a win. Finish every session by asking for the easiest skill and paying it big. The last rep is the one the puppy remembers, and a string of good endings makes the crate-to-training transition fight-free.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a dog training session be?

Two or three minutes, several times a day. A puppy's focus runs out fast, and short frequent sessions beat one long one. If the puppy checks out at 90 seconds, stop there and come back later; that is a 2-minute session, not a failure.

What should I write in a training log?

Date, skill, minutes, and a result note. The note carries the value: rep counts, whether a lure was still needed, which marker (P, R, or X) applies, and one decision for tomorrow's session.

How do I know when a skill is reliable?

First cue, no lure, several days in a row: that is an R. Until then it is P, practice. X means the session was one step too hard; go back to an easier version next time instead of repeating the hard one.

What treats should I use?

Small soft pieces about the size of a pea, counted as part of the day's food so training does not add weight. For food choices, amounts, and diet changes, ask your vet.

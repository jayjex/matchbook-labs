Cold Email Prompt: Free Fill-In Template (2026)

A cold email is a short note to a stranger who owes you nothing, and the prompt behind it does most of the work. This free guide gives a six-line fill-in AI cold email template, one complete example, a one-line rule for follow-ups, and four opinions on writing cold email prompts. The prompt pack it links to sits at the bottom.

How it works: fill the six lines, paste the prompt into your AI tool of choice, read the draft it returns, and edit before anything is sent. The template below is generic on purpose; nothing in it is tied to a product, industry, or tool.

The six lines a cold email prompt needs

Each line is a write-in. The example fills come from the worked example further down, so you can see one full set end to end.

LineWhat to fill inExample fill
Who (write-in)Who the email is from and who it is for: a name or role on each side, so the tool writes between two real parties.from a freelance logo designer to the owner of a bike shop opening next month
What (write-in)What you are offering or asking about, in one sentence a stranger can follow.a simple logo refresh before opening week
Why them (write-in)The specific reason this recipient gets this email and not someone else.their opening-day post says the sign art is still a placeholder
Ask (write-in)Exactly one action you want from the recipient.a yes or no on two rough concepts by Friday
LengthHow long the email should run, set as a cap the prompt can hold the output to.under 120 words
Sign-offHow the email ends, named in the prompt so the tool does not invent one.first name and a portfolio link

The fill-in template

Copy this block, replace the brackets, and paste it into your AI tool. The six lines from the table map straight onto it.

You are writing a cold email from [WHO_YOU_ARE] to [RECIPIENT].

The email is about [WHAT], in one sentence a stranger can follow.

Why this recipient: [WHY_THEM], the specific thing they built, posted, or run that connects to the ask.

The ask: [ASK]. Exactly one action.

Format: the whole email fits in [LENGTH].

Constraint: plain sentences, no filler openers, and sign off with [SIGN_OFF].

One worked example

The same prompt with all six lines filled, verbatim. The scenario is generic: a freelance logo designer writing to a shop owner.

You are writing a cold email from a freelance logo designer to the owner of a bike shop that opens next month.

The email is about a simple logo refresh: the shop's current sign art is a placeholder and opening week is close.

Why this recipient: their opening-day post says the sign is still being finished, so the timing is real.

The ask: a yes or no on two rough concepts by Friday.

Format: the whole email fits in under 120 words.

Constraint: plain sentences, no filler openers, and sign off with a first name and a portfolio link.

Follow-up note: send one short follow-up a few days later that references the first email, and treat the follow-up as its own prompt run with the thread pasted in.

Four opinions on cold email prompts

These are opinions from writing and reusing prompts, not survey data and not claims about any specific tool.

  1. (Opinion) One ask per email. An email with two asks makes the reader pick between tasks, and a busy reader resolves that by answering neither.
  2. (Opinion) Write the why-them line like you looked at their work. A why-them that could be sent to anyone tells the reader the other hundred words could be too.
  3. (Opinion) Put the ask in the first three lines. The reason can come second; a stranger reading on a phone may never reach line nine.
  4. (Opinion) Spend prompt words on what to avoid. Naming the failure modes in the prompt (no filler openers, no attachments on the first email, no invented sign-off) is cheaper than fixing them in the output.

A shift swap log turns "can you take my Tuesday?" from a hallway conversation into a row on paper. This printable holds twelve swaps on one sheet: the date, the alias handing the shift off, the alias picking it up, the shift itself, a circle for the answer, and initials. Print one per team and tape it where the roster lives, so "wait, who's covering Tuesday?" gets answered by pointing at the sheet. How to use this page: print at 100% scale, one sheet. Write your workplace's own swap rules into the rules box first, in your words. Log a swap once it's agreed: date, the alias of whoever hands the shift off, the alias of whoever takes it, and the shift. Circle Y or N in the approved column only when the answer exists, then initials from whoever confirmed it. A blank circle means pending. Twelve rows full? Print another sheet.

The one-page shift swap log

Everything on the sheet is write-in on purpose: no preset rules, no policy text, no names. The rules box stays empty until someone writes the workplace's own swap rules into it, three lines: who approves a swap, how much notice it needs, which shifts can move. Aliases, not names, because the sheet lives in the open near the roster; names belong in the personnel record, which stays somewhere locked. The shift column takes whatever your roster calls a shift: a day code, a time range, or both.

The approved circle only marks an answer that was already given somewhere real, and the initials say who gave it. The sheet records swaps; it does not approve them and it does not settle disagreements. When the same shift keeps changing hands, the twelve rows make that visible fast, which is most of what a log does that memory can't.

4 opinions on running a swap log

  1. Approve the swap before shift day, while a schedule change is still cheap. (Opinion.) A swap agreed the night before and confirmed the morning of leaves the incoming alias no time to prep and the roster out of date at the worst hour. The circle goes in before the shift arrives, or the swap waits. A row logged without a circle is the sheet flagging its own loose ends.
  2. Write the rules box before the first swap. (Opinion.) Three lines: who says yes, how much notice, which shifts can move. A log with no rules box turns every dispute into a memory contest, and the two people arguing rarely remember the same conversation. The box is write-in because swap rules belong to the workplace, and yours are the ones that count.
  3. Initials close the row. (Opinion.) Date and aliases say what was agreed; initials say a person confirmed it. A row without initials is a conversation; a row with them is a record. Whoever runs the roster initials, and the circle and initials go in the same minute.
  4. Aliases on the sheet, names in the file. (Opinion.) The README's own instruction for the personnel record: it holds personal data, so store it somewhere locked. A swap log works taped by the time clock or on a clipboard, and aliases keep it readable in public. J.D. to R.O. means everything to the crew and nothing to a visitor, which is the point.

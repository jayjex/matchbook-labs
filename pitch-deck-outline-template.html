Pitch Deck Outline Template

Ten slides, one job each, in the order most startup decks run. Treat it as a skeleton: draft every slide against its row, cut what doesn't earn its place, and keep the appendix for everything else. There's a companion slide pack at the end if you'd rather start from finished layouts.

The 10-slide order

#SlideIts job
1TitleWho you are and what this deck covers. Company name, product line, done. Everything else is a subtitle at best.
2ProblemThe pain you fix, phrased the way the person living it would describe it. Specific beats systemic.
3SolutionYour fix in one sentence, plus the smallest proof it exists: a screenshot, a demo frame, a before/after.
4MarketWho has the problem and how many of them there are. Bottom-up counts read more credible than big top-down circles.
5TractionWhatever you have today: users, revenue, pilots, waitlist signups. Early numbers count even when they're small.
6Business modelWho pays, how much, how often. If pricing isn't decided yet, show the range you're testing.
7CompetitionWho else solves this and where you sit. A simple matrix reads faster than a paragraph of comparisons.
8TeamThe people building it and the connection between their background and this exact problem.
9The askWhat you're raising, what it buys, how long it lasts. Numbers, not vibes.
10ContactOne email and one link. When a deck gets forwarded, the last slide is the part that always comes along.

Four opinions, not rules

  • Ten slides is a ceiling, not a floor (opinion). A deck gets skimmed, not studied. If a slide doesn't change what the reader thinks, it's appendix material.
  • The ask needs a number (opinion). "We're exploring a round" reads as "we haven't decided". A number makes the next conversation concrete, even if it changes later.
  • Show traction as a chart, however small (opinion). A bar chart with 40 users tells a sharper story than the phrase "growing fast". Charts also age better than adjectives.
  • Draft the problem slide last (opinion). After cutting the deck to ten slides you know the real pain. Writing the problem first usually means writing the company's internal pitch to itself.

Three common mistakes

Feature dump. Nine features on one slide means none of them get remembered. One capability per slide, the rest to the appendix.

No ask number. A request without a figure isn't an ask, it's a mood. Whatever the number is, put it on the slide.

Wall of text. Decks get read on a screen while someone talks, or forwarded without anyone talking. Paragraphs die in both settings; short lines and labels survive.

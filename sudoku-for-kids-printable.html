How to play 4x4 sudoku (4 steps):

1. Each row must contain the digits 1, 2, 3 and 4 exactly once. No repeats.

2. Each column must also contain 1, 2, 3 and 4 exactly once.

3. Each of the four thick-lined 2x2 boxes must contain 1, 2, 3 and 4 exactly once.

4. The numbers already printed are the clues. Fill every empty cell so all three rules hold at the same time. A finished grid shows each digit four times, once per row, column, and box.

When your kid is ready for bigger grids

The usual next step after 4x4 is a 4x6 grid: 4 rows, 6 columns, digits 1 to 6, and six 2x3 boxes. Nothing new to learn, just one more digit to keep track of, which is why the jump works. After that comes 6x6, then the classic 9x9 that adults do. If a 4x4 still takes more than ten minutes, print this page twice and repeat puzzles 1 and 2 before moving up. Speed is not the goal; getting the three rules to hold in their head at once is the actual skill.

3 tips for kids learning sudoku

Opinions from watching kids do these, not a teaching study:

  1. Scan a full row before writing anything. Kids want to fill the first empty cell they see. Teach them to point at a row, say the missing number out loud ("this row needs a 3"), then look at that number's column and box before writing. Saying it out loud is the part that sticks; it forces the check.
  2. Pencil marks: write small candidates in the corner. When a cell could be a 2 or a 3, write both tiny in the corner and move on. Coming back later, one of the two will now be impossible, and crossing out a tiny pencil number feels much better than erasing a wrong answer in ink. On 4x4 grids this habit is learnable; on 9x9 it is required.
  3. Start with the busiest box, not the top-left. The box with the most clues gives the most free answers, and free answers early keep a kid in the game. In puzzle 2 above that is the bottom-left box. Chasing hard cells first is how sudoku gets a reputation as "boring" in about ninety seconds.

Common questions

What age is 4x4 sudoku good for?

Ages 5 to 9 in practice: a five-year-old usually needs the row-scanning help from tip 1, while an eight-year-old races all three in under ten minutes. Below age 5 the two-rules-at-once part is usually too much; counting practice lands better at that age.

Do these puzzles have exactly one solution?

Yes, all three. They were generated by a program that removes clues only while a solver confirms the grid still has exactly one solution, and each finished grid was re-checked before publishing. A puzzle with two valid answers teaches the wrong lesson, so this check matters.

Is sudoku for kids different from adult sudoku?

The rules are identical, only the grid is smaller: 4x4 with four 2x2 boxes instead of 9x9 with nine 3x3 boxes. Everything a kid learns here, row and column scanning, box checking, pencil marks, transfers straight to the big grid later.

Page 1: the five breakdown rows

One goal at a time. Each row asks one question; the answer goes on the line. If a question stalls you for more than a minute, that's the row to sit with. The goal isn't ready until all five rows have answers.

Four opinions on using these sheets

  1. (Opinion.) One goal per sheet. Two pages sound generous until you try to fit three goals into them. Five real answers for one goal beat fifteen half-answers across three.
  2. (Opinion.) Milestones pass a yes/no test. "Book the exam" can be checked done. "Get better at math" can't. If you can't picture telling someone "done" and being believed, the milestone is too vague.
  3. (Opinion.) Date first, milestone second. Write the date before the milestone text. An undated milestone is a someday list, and the done circle means nothing without a date next to it.
  4. (Opinion.) Four rows is the cap on purpose. A goal with more than four real checkpoints usually has steps small enough to sit inside another one. Fold them in and keep the table scannable in five seconds.

A planning sheet. It organizes one goal onto two pages: what the goal is, what the five breakdown questions pull out of it, and which four checkpoints mark progress.

Etsy Listing Photos Guide

A listing photo set is easier to plan as a list of jobs than as a number of pictures. Each slot answers one buyer question: what is it, how big, how it lives in a room, what arrives in the mail. This page lays out the six jobs in one table, adds two short notes on light and background, and closes with four opinions, labeled as such.

How to use it: shoot the table top to bottom for one product in one session before touching the camera settings twice. The six slots cover most physical products; skip a row only when it genuinely does not apply to yours.

The 6-shot list

ShotWhat it does
Hero shotThe whole product, filling most of the frame, on one clean surface. It answers "what am I looking at" in under a second, because most buyers decide on this frame whether to read anything else.
Detail shotOne feature up close: a stitch, a grain, a switch, a texture. It answers "what is it made of" and shows the kind of care a full-frame view washes out.
Scale shotThe product next to a familiar object: a hand, a doorway, a coffee mug. It answers "how big is it really", which a lone product photo cannot, since a camera carries no sense of size.
Lifestyle shotThe product in use, in the room or routine it belongs to. It answers "where does this fit in my life" and lets a buyer picture owning it instead of viewing it.
Packaging shotThe box, wrap, or pouch as it ships. It answers "what arrives", which matters for gifts, and quietly signals that shipping was planned rather than improvised.
Video frameA short clip of the product moving: a hinge working, fabric shifting, a lid closing. Video answers the questions still photos dodge, like how the item behaves when handled.

Light, in one note

Window light beats most of what you can buy for a home studio. Put the table near a window, turn off the room lights, and shoot from the side the window faces so the product is lit and the camera is not staring into the glass. Midday sun through thin curtains gives you the soft version; direct sun gives hard shadows, so move the table or wait an hour. One light source from one direction keeps every frame in the set looking like it came from the same day.

Background, in one note

Pick one background per set and keep it out of the way: a plain wall, a tabletop, a single sheet of poster board curved into a sweep. The product should hold every point of contrast in the frame; a busy surface steals that job. If you want a second background for the lifestyle row, that is fine, but the hero, detail, and scale rows belong on the same one, so the set reads as one product and not six listings.

4 opinion tips

  1. Shoot an odd number of photos. (Opinion.) Five or seven frames force a choice: which one is the hero, and what does each of the others do. A round ten invites padding, and padding reads as filler. This is a habit, not a rule; the shot list above is the actual checklist.
  2. The first photo gets one job. (Opinion.) I would rather lose a clever detail frame than have the hero frame carrying text, props, and the product at once. A thumbnail is skimmed at the size of a stamp, and a busy first frame shrinks to noise.
  3. Reshoot when the light moved, not when the shot is imperfect. (Opinion.) Shadows creeping between the third and seventh photo tell buyers the set was assembled over days. An imperfect frame from one consistent session looks more trustworthy than a flawless set with drifting light.
  4. Take the packaging shot first. (Opinion.) Once the product is out and styled, nobody wants to unpack and re-shoot the box. Shooting what ships while the mail is still fresh on the table gets the row done without a second session.

What this page is

This is a photography-craft page: a shot list, two technique notes, and opinions. Etsy is named only to identify the platform whose listing photo slots the table serves, and this site is unaffiliated with Etsy. Nothing here interprets any marketplace policy; photo composition is ordinary seller craft. No line on this page promises views, traffic, or sales. Buyers respond to pictures you take of the product you actually have, and the honest position is to photograph what ships and label what does not.

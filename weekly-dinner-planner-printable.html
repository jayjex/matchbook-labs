Why dinner gets its own page

Dinner is the meal with opinions in it. Other people have preferences, schedules collide, and the main dish is the decision everything else hangs on. The all-meals grid spreads 21 cells across the week and works well for that; this page goes narrow instead: seven dinners, each with a side and a prep note. Fewer cells to fill means the ones that exist get filled. A dinner plan with empty cells still beats a full grid nobody finished.

The prep-note column is the quiet win

The main and side decide what; the prep note decides whether it goes smoothly. Three words is the right size: the reminder that moves work earlier, names the missing ingredient, or hands a night to someone else. The column exists because the plan is read at 5pm by whoever is tired, and the difference between a note and no note is whether that person starts from a decision or from a blank.

Four dinner-planning opinions

Opinions from running tables like this one, take or leave:

  1. Cook double once. Opinion: one dinner a week is worth cooking double, and the repeat gets written into a later row as "repeat" or "double from Tue". One cooking session covers two planned nights, and the hardest part of the week shrinks by a row. Pick whichever night deserves the break and plan the repeat there on purpose.
  2. Plan the busiest day first. Opinion: the hardest evening decides the shape of the whole week, so it gets written before the easy ones. Once the busiest row holds something achievable, the other six fill themselves around it, and the plan survives the week instead of dying on its heaviest day.
  3. Let the side repeat. Opinion: a main that varies with a side that repeats all week is a legitimate plan. Every repeated side is one decision a day you no longer make, and the side is rarely what anyone remembers about dinner anyway. Write it once, copy it down, and spend the saved thinking on the mains.
  4. Write prep notes to tomorrow. Opinion: the column is a message to the cook at 5pm, not a plan. "Start early", "shop first", "ask Dad to grill" do more than any schedule, because they arrive exactly when the tired person needs them. If a note needs a second sentence, it belongs somewhere else.

What this page does not do

It organizes dinners; it says nothing about what anyone should eat. There are no recipes on the page (every cell is write-in), no nutrition guidance, and no food-safety guidance of any kind. Those are household decisions the sheet never touches. What it does do is put seven dinner rows, three grocery runs, and one leftover night on a single page, so the week's hardest meal runs on a plan instead of a 5pm mood.

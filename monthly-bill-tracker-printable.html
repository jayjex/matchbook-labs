Monthly Bill Tracker Printable

A bill tracker does one job: put every recurring bill, its due day, and its paid status on one sheet you actually see. This page has a complete tracker to print (12 bills, a due-day circle covering every date from 1 to 31, a write-in amount, and a paid checkbox), a note on the two ways people track payments, a box for the annual bills that skip months, and four tracking habits that keep the sheet honest. The cash envelope printable handles the spending side; this one handles the fixed side. Everything here is free and prints on one page.

How to use this page: print at 100% scale (the site chrome drops out on paper). Write the month at the top, list up to 12 bills, circle each bill's due day, and mark the paid box as each payment clears. Tape it inside a cabinet door or stick it to the fridge. New month, new sheet: print another.

The tracker: one page, 12 bills

Two rows come filled in as examples so the columns make sense at a glance; yours will differ. The due-day strip covers every date from 1 to 31, so a bill that lands on the 31st has a home, and months without a 31st simply leave it uncircled that month. Amounts stay write-in so the sheet works no matter what the statement says this month.

Autopay and manual pay: two ways people track it

The tracker works with either setup, and plenty of households run both at once:

  • Autopay. The bill clears itself on the scheduled day. On the sheet, the paid box still gets marked, because the point of the row is the visual record: a glance says this month's electricity went through without opening a banking app. Some people pre-mark the box and review the statement against it later; others wait for the charge to land. Either way the row stays on the sheet.
  • Manual pay. You pay it, on a day you pick, and the sheet is the reminder. The circled due day is the deadline, the paid box is the finish line. This is the setup where the sheet earns its spot on the fridge.

Mixed is common: autopay for the flat, never-changes bills, manual for the ones that swing month to month, so somebody actually looks at the amount before it clears.

The annual ones: the box that saves the sheet

Most forgotten bills are not monthly ones. They are the bills that show up once or twice a year on their own schedule, which is exactly why they fall off a monthly sheet. Four habits that make the sheet work

Opinions from printing and running this sheet, take or leave:

  1. Same spot, always. The tracker lives in one physical place: taped inside a cabinet door, stuck to the fridge, clipped to a corkboard. A tracker in a drawer is a tracker that stopped existing. The one-rule version of bill tracking is "same spot for the mail and the sheet."
  2. Calendar reminder 3 days early. Paper handles the list; a phone reminder three days before the earliest circled date handles the nudge. Three days buys enough room to fix a failed payment without eating into the weekend.
  3. Fill the sheet the day a bill arrives. New statement shows up in the mail or inbox, the row gets its amount written immediately, thirty seconds. Doing it later means doing it never, and the amount is the whole point of looking.
  4. Month-end review, two minutes. Last day of the month: every paid box marked, every amount roughly matches what cleared. Two minutes. The review is also when next month's blank sheet gets printed, so the new month starts full instead of empty.

About late fees

Late-fee rules are set by whoever issued the bill, and they differ: some charge a flat amount, some a percentage, some nothing the first time. The sheet does not try to predict any of that. Instead, there is a line for it: write your card's or biller's fee policy once at the bottom of the tracker (flat fee, percentage, grace days, whatever your statement says), and the number is there when a payment runs late. The statement or biller agreement is the source; the sheet is just where you keep the answer.

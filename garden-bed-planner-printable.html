Four layout tips

Opinions from making garden-organization pages like this one. Take or leave.

  1. Tall plants on the north edge. (Opinion.) The folk rule: put the tallest crops on the north side of the bed so they stop shading everything else, and flip it if you garden below the equator. Whether it matters in your yard is something your own shade lines will tell you by mid-season, which is what the notes lines are for.
  2. Name beds like a person, not a spreadsheet. (Opinion.) "Bed 3" goes stale; "the bed by the fence" survives years of rearranging. The bed label line exists so the grid stays readable when you find it in a drawer next spring.
  3. Re-sowings get their own row. (Opinion.) Merging two sowings of one crop into a single succession row hides the one thing the table exists to show: the gap between the dates. Two rows, two dates, no memory required.
  4. Keep the used grids. (Opinion.) Last year's grid is next year's layout, already drawn, with the crop rotations and the shade problems marked in your own handwriting. Stack them into the garden binder next to the planting log; a new season's plan is mostly the old one with edits.

Print tips

  • One sheet per bed covers most small gardens. Print one per bed section if a bed runs longer than 4 squares.
  • Print at 100% scale on A4 or US Letter. Grayscale is fine; the layout is lines and boxes, no ink-heavy fills.
  • Hole-punch straight into the garden binder. The tables are full-width with standard borders, so they punch cleanly on both paper sizes.

Four tips for the maintenance rhythm

Opinions from a log that stuck, not survey data. Take what fits your household.

  1. Pick the same weekend every quarter. The clock-change weekends work; so does the first weekend of a new quarter. The log tells you what is due, and the anchored weekend tells you when to look at the log. A maintenance schedule without a standing date is a wish list.
  2. Write the next-due date before the tools go away. The last-done date records history; the next-due date is what schedules the next visit. Fill it in while the job is fresh, and the sheet keeps working without you remembering anything.
  3. Batch the five-minute jobs. Alarm tests, filter peeks, and under-sink glances go in one thirty-minute session instead of five separate interruptions. The log carries the rest; the batch is for the small stuff that would otherwise never get a line.
  4. Reprint when a page fills up, and keep the old one. A completed log is a record of what was done and when, which is exactly what a contractor or a buyer asks for. Date the reprint, file the old sheet, and start the new one with the frequencies you already settled on.

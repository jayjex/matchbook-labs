Four opinions on invoicing

These are opinions from sending invoices, not survey data and not legal guidance. Take what fits your clients and contracts.

  1. Pick an invoice number format and never reuse a number. INV-001, INV-002 beats inventing a scheme per client: zero-pad so lists sort right, keep it sequential, and retire a number for good once it is on paper, even if you void that invoice. A repeated number is the fastest way to confuse a payer who files by it.
  2. Write a calendar date, not "net 30". "Due Sep 30" needs no decoding by whoever pays you, and it puts a real day on the sheet you can plan around. Fourteen or thirty days both work, and shorter windows rarely speed anyone up by much, so pick one window and keep it the same for every client.
  3. Keep the first late note short and warm. Assume the email got buried: one line with the invoice number and amount, the original invoice attached again. Most late invoices close after that first nudge, and the flat, friendly tone is easier to keep sending than a stiff one.
  4. Fill every field before it leaves. A blank rate, date, or terms line becomes a question, and every question adds a day. Two minutes checking the sheet beats a week of back-and-forth.

When to send it

Send the invoice the same day you deliver the work. The delivery is still fresh on both sides, so approval questions get answered in minutes instead of days, and the due clock starts while the project is still open in the client's mind. If you batch invoices at month end, the clock starts weeks after the client already has the work. Same day, every time.

Puzzle 1 — Six Easy Rows Easy

The five families

Each row follows exactly one of these patterns. The printed numbers pin down which one.

  • Same step: add or subtract the same number every move (4, 7, 10, 13...)
  • Same multiple: multiply or halve every move (3, 6, 12, 24...)
  • Growing steps: the gap itself grows by a fixed amount, like gaps of 1, 2, 3, 4 or 3, 5, 7, 9
  • Two tracks: the odd and even positions each run their own add-or-subtract line (1, 20, 3, 18...)
  • Build-up: each number is the sum of the two before it (1, 1, 2, 3, 5...)

How to solve a number sequence in 3 steps

  1. Read the row and mark the gaps between printed cells. Two blanks in a row mean you lean on the end cells; a blank between two givens usually falls out in one move.
  2. Test the families in order: same step first, then growing steps, then two tracks, then build-up, then same multiple. The first family that fits every printed cell wins. If two families seem to fit, hunt for a cell that separates them, like the printed 12 in row 6 above.
  3. Write your fills, then re-read the finished row against the family. A pattern that explains three cells can still break at the fifth, and the re-read catches it for the cost of ten seconds.

Four opinions from building these

  • (Opinion.) Solve the rows out of order. Start with the row where printed cells sit closest together. Momentum from one quick win carries you through the stubborn rows better than row order does.
  • (Opinion.) Pencil small guesses above the blanks. Holding two candidate gaps in your head while you check the next cell is where copying errors start. Let the paper carry them and erase as cells resolve.
  • (Opinion.) Print the page instead of solving on screen. A paper copy lets you cross out a whole family with one pencil line and keep the sheet as scratch work. On screen that all happens in your head, which is the weaker place for it.
  • (Opinion.) Skip the timer on the Medium set. Halving and two-track rows reward staring. A clock pushes people into arithmetic slips on exactly the rows that punish them hardest.

Checked by a script before publishing

Every row on this page went through a short Python script before it went up. The script builds every sequence the five families can produce (same step, same multiple, growing steps, two tracks, build-up), keeps the ones that match the printed cells, and counts the distinct fills. All twelve rows came back with exactly one fill. Three Easy rows answer to two family names at once, because a straight +3 line is also two parallel tracks moving together, but both readings produce the same numbers.

Interview Follow-Up Template (Copy and Send)

One thank-you email you can adapt in five minutes, the four dates that decide whether a follow-up helps or hurts, and a small tracker so nothing slips between rounds. The timing here reflects common practice, not an official rule. If the interviewer told you "we'll get back to you by Friday," their date wins over everything on this page.

The four dates: day 0, 3, 10, 21

WhenWhat happens
Day 0Interview day. Before you leave the call or the room, write down the two specifics you'd want to mention in a follow-up: one thing they said, one thing you did. Memory of a good interview decays faster than people expect.
Day 3Thank-you email. Send it within the first three days; the template below is the one to send. One message to one person per conversation, in the same thread if you already have one.
Day 10Check-in. Only if they gave you no date, or that date has passed. Two lines: still interested, is there an update on timing? Nothing attached, nothing demanded.
Day 21Move on. Three weeks of silence after a check-in usually means the role moved on without you, sometimes because the loop itself stalled. Send one last short note, then put the row in your tracker to sleep and spend the attention on new applications. Roles do reopen; you can re-apply then.

Day 10 is the one most people skip, and in my experience it's the cheapest to send. The worst realistic outcome is a "no update yet" reply, which costs you a minute to read.

The template

Replace every [bracket] before sending. A follow-up left half-filled reads worse than no follow-up at all, because the recipient can tell it's a form letter that got away from you. The body runs about 120 words, which fits the three jobs it has: thank them, add one piece of value they didn't get in the room, and close without pressure.

Subject: Thank you — [Role] interview

Hi [name],

Thanks for the time you gave the [role] conversation. The part that stuck with me was [one specific thing they said], and I've been thinking about how it fits what your team is trying to do this quarter.

I didn't get to mention it in the room, so I'll say it here. Last quarter I [one relevant project or result], and the setup is close to what you described on the [team/topic] side. Happy to go deeper if that's useful.

Also, the candid answer on [second topic] was appreciated. Job posts rarely give you that level of detail.

No rush on your end. I'm free [day] or [day] if a follow-up conversation fits the loop.

Thanks again,
[Your name]

Two adjustments worth making: if the interviewer gave you a reply date, swap the last paragraph for a simple "looking forward to hearing back by [their date]". If you spoke to several people, send each of them a version with their own specifics in the first paragraph.

Four follow-up mistakes

  1. The desperate tone. Stacking apologies ("sorry to bother you again"), restating your interest three times, or asking "did I do something wrong" turns a status check into a plea. In my view the confident version is shorter: state the facts, ask one question, stop. The email above is roughly the maximum length that still reads calm.
  2. Running every channel at once. Email, LinkedIn message, recruiter text, and a phone call in the same week reads as pressure, not enthusiasm. Pick one channel, usually whoever owns your process, and stay in it. If you email, reply in the existing thread rather than starting a fresh one, so the history stays attached.
  3. Nudging before the date they gave. When the interviewer says "end of next week," that's their process talking, and a check-in on day two asks them to break it for you. Wait until the date passes by a business day or two, then send the day-10 style note.
  4. One generic message for every interviewer. "It was a pleasure speaking with you" plus a pasted paragraph could have gone to any company, and hiring managers compare notes. The value recap in the template exists precisely so each email carries something the other candidates' emails don't.

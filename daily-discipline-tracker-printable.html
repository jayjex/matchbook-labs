Six commitment rows with a circle to fill per day, an evening row for what you kept and what you missed, and a streak counter for the days every row held. Pen and paper, a couple of minutes a night. How to use this page: print at 100% and write your commitments on Sunday evening or Monday morning, before the week starts. The first two rows are grayed-out examples ("wake time", "no-spend"): write over them or replace them. Each evening, fill the day's circle only for the rows you actually kept, then write one line in the evening row. Fill the streak counter circle only on days every commitment held. One sheet covers one week; print a fresh copy for the next.

The layout makes one evening pass possible. Circles sit in the same row as the commitment they belong to, so a fill is one glance and one pen stroke, not a hunt across the page. The evening row is separate from the circles on purpose: a circle says a row was kept, the written line says what actually happened, and the two disagree often enough that both are worth having. The streak counter takes all-or-nothing days because partial credit is what the circles above already record.

Four tips for running the sheet

Opinions from printing and filling these sheets, not findings from anywhere - your rows and your weeks will differ.

  1. Shrink each commitment until it's boring. "Walk 10 minutes" survives a bad Tuesday; a row that needs a good day only gets filled on good days. Six dull rows that fill every circle beat six ambitious ones that fill two. That trade is the whole page, in my opinion from ruining plenty of week sheets the other way.
  2. Write commitments a stranger could grade. "Tidy the desk before bed" is checkable; "be more organized" turns every evening into an argument with yourself. If a row can't fail, it can't fill a circle either. The habit streak tracker page works the same way if three habits is your scale.
  3. Fill the evening row that night, not the next morning. Same-night lines record the day; next-morning lines record your mood about the day. The "tomorrow, one thing" column is the one that pays: it hands morning-you a decision already made.
  4. Miss a circle, keep the sheet. An empty circle costs one cell. Dropping the sheet for the week because Monday went sideways costs seven days of records. Fill what happened, including the misses, and start the next week on a fresh print.

One week or ninety days?

This sheet answers "how did this week go". The 90-day challenge tracker answers "how did the quarter go", with a 90-box grid, weekly reviews and checkpoints at day 30, 60 and 90. A reasonable sequence: run this page for two or three weeks, keep the commitments that filled their circles, then take the survivors into the longer grid. The monthly habit tracker sits between the two if 31 days is the span you want.

Honest note: the sheet is a record of what happened. Filling circles feels good and missing them stings, and neither feeling does the next day's push for you; that stays on you. Nothing on this page is a claim about psychology or outcomes, the tips are opinions from use, and the only thing the tracker guarantees is that the week stops being a blur.

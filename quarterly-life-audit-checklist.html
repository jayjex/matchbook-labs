Free Quarterly Life Audit Checklist (Two Pages)

A quarterly life audit is the boring kind of audit: once a quarter, you rate each part of your life 1 to 5, write one line about why, and decide what to keep and what to change before the next quarter starts. Most people never put those answers on the same sheet of paper. These two printable pages hold them. Page 1 is an eight-row table - each row gets an area name you write in (work, home, money, health, or whatever you're tracking this quarter), a 1-5 rating circle, and a note line. Page 2 is a keep-change-start box, a next-quarter box, and nothing else. Print it, write on it, reprint it next quarter. Below the sheet sits a $16 checklist for a different kind of audit entirely - smart-contract code, not your life.

How to use this page: print at 100% scale - both pages come out on two sheets. Do page 1 in one sitting, about ten minutes: write an area name on each row, circle a rating for each, and write the one-line reason in the note column. Don't average the scores - eight separate circles tell you more than one blended number. Page 2 takes another five minutes and works best a day later, once the page-1 ratings have had time to settle.

4 opinions on running this quarterly review

  1. Keep the same eight rows all year. (Opinion.) Tempting to rename the areas when one gets awkward - "money" becomes "finances" becomes "career + money" - and each rename breaks the comparison with the quarter before. The value of a quarterly life audit comes from asking the same questions four times, not from the questions themselves. Pick the eight, keep them four quarters, then renegotiate.
  2. Circle the number first, before any note. (Opinion.) If you write the note first, you'll talk yourself into the rating the paragraph supports, and the circle becomes a summary of your mood the day you filled the sheet. The number you circle cold, before explaining it, is the one that matches the quarter. The note line is for the reason - it can disagree with the circle, and that disagreement is worth reading next quarter.
  3. Fill page 2 the same week, not the same hour. (Opinion.) The ratings are answers; the keep-change-start lines are commitments, and they deserve a separate sitting. A start-line written ten minutes after a bad rating is usually a reaction. A day later, the same start-line is usually a plan.
  4. Reprint fresh each quarter and keep the old sheets. (Opinion.) Editing one sheet forever turns the audit into a diary. A fresh print each quarter, with the previous sheet filed next to it, shows the drift you'd never notice week to week: which number moved, which start-line finally got used, whether the next review date on the old sheet actually happened.

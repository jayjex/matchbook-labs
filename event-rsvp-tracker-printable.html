This event RSVP tracker prints on two sheets and covers one gathering end to end: a ten-row guest list with a party-size column and an RSVP circle for yes, no or maybe on the front, then a headcount total and a dietary notes box on the back. Pen and paper. Hosting is only half the calendar problem; if you also need to plan which gatherings to attend, the event prep checklist covers the other side.

How to use this page: print both sheets at 100% and keep them together. Write the event name and date on the front, then one guest per row: name, party size, and the Y, N or M circle you fill in when their reply lands. Use the notes column for chased-on dates or seating thoughts. On the back, total the party sizes into a headcount and keep dietary notes next to it. The two boxes sit on one page because they get used on the same day: the day you order food and chairs. Line two of the headcount box is deliberately a judgment call, and writing the number down makes it a decision you can revisit instead of a feeling. The dietary lines are for what you need to buy or avoid, not a medical form; guests with serious allergies should confirm details with you directly.

Four tips for running the list

Opinions from printing and filling sheets like this one, not findings from anywhere - your guests and your rows will differ.

  1. (Opinion.) Count party sizes, not names. One name on the list can walk in with three people, which is why the party column exists and why it takes a number even for a solo guest. Add the column on the back sheet when the list closes; a headcount built from names alone is the reason chairs run out.
  2. (Opinion.) Give the maybes a deadline. The M circle is where guest lists stall, so pick a date about a week before the event, write it in the notes column of every M row, and chase them on that date. A maybe with no deadline is a no with worse manners.
  3. (Opinion.) Fill the circle the day the reply lands. Replies arrive by text, in person, secondhand through someone else; a circle filled same-day beats a remembered answer. The notes column is where the trail lives: "chased 9/12", "said yes via Sam". When the sheet and your memory disagree, the sheet wins.
  4. (Opinion.) Keep the dietary box with the headcount, not in the kitchen. Food gets ordered the same day the final number gets set, and separate lists drift apart. Three lines is enough because the box is a pointer, not a database: write the guest's initial next to the restriction so you can ask a follow-up without digging through the front page.

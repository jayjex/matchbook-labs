Airbnb Occupancy Rate Data: Reading availability_365 Across 6 US Markets

Anyone hunting for airbnb occupancy rate data hits the same wall: no public source publishes booked nights. What exists instead is availability_365, a calendar column that works as the standard proxy. This page explains how to read it, what it can't tell you, and what the numbers look like across a 90,169-listing short term rental occupancy CSV covering six US cities.

Why There Is No Occupancy Column

Airbnb doesn't release booking counts or revenue, and Inside Airbnb's scrapes don't capture them either. What each scrape does capture is the listing's public calendar as it stood on crawl day. That calendar is stored in a column named availability_365.

The data dictionary in our pack defines it exactly: "Nights available in the next 365 days per the scrape-day calendar. A proxy for how active or blocked the calendar is."

The two ends of the scale are easy to read. A listing at availability_365 = 10 has nearly every night blocked out. A listing at 350 is open most of the year. Neither number tells you how many nights guests actually stayed, and that gap is where most misuse of this column happens.

Three Ways to Work With the Column

  • Seasonality scan, as a cross-section - sort one city's listings by availability_365 and look at the clusters. Listings near 0 have blocked-out calendars; listings near 365 stay open year-round. One snapshot gives you a cross-section, not a curve; a seasonal curve needs repeated scrapes over months. Pairing the column with minimum_nights also surfaces mid-term inventory fast, since 30-night minimums stand out immediately.
  • Supply density map - coordinates are rounded to 4 decimal places (about 11 meters) and each row carries a cleansed neighbourhood name. Plot listing counts per area, then filter on availability_365 above a threshold you pick, and you get two different maps: where listings exist, and where open calendars cluster.
  • Availability benchmark per city - the per-city averages above (190 to 251) set a baseline. When you compute an average for any subset, a neighbourhood, a room type, a price band, you can compare it against the whole market it came from instead of eyeballing in a vacuum.

All three are analysis patterns for data you already have. None of them involve a booking decision, a revenue projection, or advice of any kind; the pack ships numbers, and the interpretation is yours.

What the Proxy Cannot Tell You

Blocked does not mean booked. A night can be blocked for an owner's stay, a seasonal closure, or a host's strategy. Open does not mean empty either, since hosts edit calendars at any time after the scrape.

One snapshot per city. Source crawl window 2026-06-22 to 08-10, with each city carrying a single date from that range. Every CSV is a point-in-time scrape, not a live feed, so treat rows as the market on that date.

Reviews lag and prices need cleaning. A listing booked this month can still show an old last_review date, and price is zero or blank on a handful of rows, so filter those before any per-night math.

Realized occupancy is not in any public dataset. If a source claims exact Airbnb occupancy rates, it's showing you a model. The honest options are repeated scrapes over time or your own booking records.

Joining All Six Cities Into One CSV

4 tips for first-time sitters

In my view, these four separate a sitter who gets rebooked from one who gets a polite thank-you and no second date.

1. Send a photo report after the visit, not a test of memory

A photo of the pet mid-walk or settled on the couch does more than any paragraph: it shows state, not claims. Pair it with the message line from the notes sheet and the owner stops wondering. Photos of medication packaging or dosing are a different matter: that is the owner's written instruction to follow and confirm in words, not a photo opportunity.

2. Ask about hiding spots before you need them

The first time a cat is missing at visit two is the worst time to learn the apartment has a gap behind the dresser. Ask on visit one: where does the pet go when scared, what sounds trigger it, and what has worked before. Write the answers on the profile. This is a five-minute question that prevents one panicked phone call later.

3. Log the short and strange days too

A 10-minute rain visit with no walk is still information: the owner learns you showed up, and you have a record if anything is questioned later. Sitters who only log the good days look like they forgot the rest.

4. Write facts, not diagnoses

"Ate half the food, left the rest" is useful. "Seemed off, probably the new food" is a guess that can send an owner down the wrong path. Notes that state what you saw, in order, let whoever reads them decide what matters.

When to write an incident note

Most visits need nothing more than the sheet above. Write a separate incident note when any of these happen: a pet is missing or unreachable for more than a few minutes, property is damaged, an escape happens at a door or gate, another animal or person is involved, or you had to make a call you did not expect to make.

The note stays generic and factual: what happened, in order; where and when exactly; who was present; who was called and who was reached; how it ended; what you promised to follow up on, with dates. Write it the same day, keep your copy, and give the owner a copy if they ask. A record written calmly at the kitchen table reads better than one reconstructed from texts a week later.

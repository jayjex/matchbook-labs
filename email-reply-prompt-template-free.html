The five lines of an email reply prompt

An email reply prompt template needs five lines. Fill them once, save the file, and only the key-points line changes from email to email.

LineWhat it doesExample fill
ToneSets how the reply sounds."Polite and direct, no exclamation marks."
Key pointsNames what the reply must say."The new date is [DATE]; here is what changed."
LengthCaps the size of the output."Three short paragraphs, under 120 words."
What to avoidBans habits you do not want."No repeated apologies, no invented dates."
Sign-offFixes the close."Thanks," then your first name.

Tone instructions fade when a thread drags; a banned phrase stays banned. And a prompt without the key-points line produces a polite answer to the wrong question, which is the most common reply failure.

The fill-in template

Brackets mark what you change. Square brackets are placeholders, not code.

Reply to the email I paste below.

Tone: [TONE_IN_YOUR_WORDS, e.g. polite and direct, no exclamation marks].

Key points: [WHAT_THE_REPLY_MUST_SAY, e.g. the new date is DATE, one line on what changed].

Length: [CAP, e.g. three short paragraphs, under 120 words total].

Do not: [HABITS_TO_BAN, e.g. apologize more than once, invent a date].

Sign-off: [YOUR_CLOSE, e.g. Thanks, then your first name].

A complete example, filled in

This is a working AI email writing prompt for replying to a client who asked about a delayed delivery. Paste the email thread under it, in the same message.

Reply to the email I paste below.

Tone: polite and direct, no exclamation marks, no filler openers.

Key points: the updated delivery date is [DATE]; the delay came from [REASON]; name one thing you are doing about it.

Length: three short paragraphs, under 120 words total.

Do not: apologize more than once, invent a date, blame a named coworker.

Sign-off: "Thanks," then your first name.

Every line does one job. Naming the key points before the thread gets pasted matters because the tool answers the questions it finds instead of the ones you care about. Naming the length keeps the reply inside what a busy reader finishes.

An output checklist for AI email replies

Run the draft through four checks before it goes out. Each one targets a failure mode reply drafts share.

  • Questions answered: every question in the original email gets an answer, in order.
  • Tone matches the tone line: a "polite and direct" prompt should not produce three exclamation marks.
  • Length holds: the draft sits inside the cap, with no filler paragraph up top.
  • The avoid-list stayed avoided: banned phrases and banned moves are absent.

A draft that fails a check goes back with the fix pointed at. "Keep the structure, answer the second question in the first paragraph" reruns in seconds.

Four opinions on email reply prompts

These are opinions from writing and reusing reply prompts, not survey data and not claims about any specific tool.

  1. (Opinion) Paste the whole thread, not just the last email. The last message alone hides what was already promised two turns ago, and a reply inconsistent with its own history reads worse than a late one. Full thread in, same message as the prompt.
  2. (Opinion) Write tone as instructions, not adjectives. "Warm" gives the tool nothing to enforce. "Polite, direct, no exclamation marks" is a tone the tool can follow and you can check afterward.
  3. (Opinion) Cap the length at what a busy reader finishes. Most replies earn three short paragraphs: the answer, one supporting detail, the next step. Anything past that belongs in an attachment or a meeting.
  4. (Opinion) Spend your prompt words on what to avoid. Banning "per my last email" and "I hope this finds you well" does more for the final draft than another sentence of praise words, because those habits survive every other instruction.

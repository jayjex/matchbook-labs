The three boxes come first because a plan that starts with the inbox never reaches priority two. The schedule grid runs the full week on one page so you can see Thursday's emptiness while you are still standing on Monday. Habit names stay small enough to fit a checkbox: "walk 20 minutes", not "get fit". Paper vs app: what each one wins

Both sides of this deserve honesty, because they win different fights. The app is better at search: a thousand notes sit one Ctrl+F away, links jump between projects, and a backup survives spilled coffee. Apps like Obsidian and Notion are good at exactly that - this site sells a template for the first one, so read that as the disclosure it is. Paper is better at friction: a printed page opens in zero seconds, runs on no battery, sends no notifications, and cannot open a second tab while you are deciding what 09:00 is for. Writing by hand also slows you down at exactly the rate that filters out the third-priority noise. A common setup uses both, and this page exists because the paper half usually goes missing: the app holds the searchable archive, and one printed sheet holds today. If your digital side runs on plain markdown, the free vault template and this page share the same skeleton - one thing first, checkbox lines, a two-minute close - so the two halves stay in the same handwriting.

Four tips for the one-page-a-day habit

Opinions, from printing pages the hard way. Your mileage will differ on the details; the order below is the order they earned.

  1. Print the week ahead. Sunday evening, print seven pages, clip them, tear one off each morning. The printing step is the actual habit - a planner you print on demand at 09:00 is a planner you skip on the days you need it.
  2. Keep an inbox tray. One physical tray next to the page. Whatever lands mid-morning - a receipt, a sticky note, mail - goes in the tray, not onto today's grid. Sort the tray once during the weekly review instead of twenty times during the day.
  3. Never reuse a page. A half-empty day page is data: it shows what got time and what quietly never did. Ripping it out and starting over throws away the only honest record of the week.
  4. Close in two minutes. Before bed, check what moved to tomorrow, then write tomorrow's top 3 while today's context is still loaded. Morning-you starts reading instead of deciding.

The weekly review: three questions

Once a week - Sunday evening pairs with the printing habit - flip back through the week's pages and answer three questions, on the back of the oldest page if nowhere else:

  1. What carried over more than twice? Delete it or give it a real time block. A third carry-over is a decision to never do it, made without deciding.
  2. Which schedule rows stayed empty all week? Those are the real free hours. Book them on purpose, or stop pretending they are work time.
  3. What does Monday's page need at the top of box 1? Write it now, while the week's wreckage is still visible.

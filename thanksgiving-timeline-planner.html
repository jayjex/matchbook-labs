The oven schedule box: 6 rows

The oven is the only appliance that has to run everything, so it gets its own box. Six rows, one per oven dish: the dish, the temperature you will run it at, the time it goes in, and the time it comes out. Fill this from the 3-hours-before and morning-of bands once the menu is set. Two dishes written at the same temperature can share the oven; two dishes at different temperatures cannot, and the schedule is where you catch that before Thursday instead of during.

Guest count to side count (opinion, not math)

People ask this every year and recipes dodge it. Here is our take, and it is a take: sides scale with the table but not one-to-one, because starches and veg share plates. These numbers assume a main plus sides, everyone eats, and nobody brought a dish. If your crowd runs heavy on sides-as-identity, add one; the table is a starting line, not a rule.

4 scheduling tips that hold every year

  1. Write times backward from dinner hour, not forward from morning. Start at the time you eat, subtract carve-and-rest for the main, and let the in-oven times fall out of that. Forward planning hides collisions; backward planning exposes them on paper.
  2. Shift the whole column, not single rows. The band times assume a 4–6 PM dinner. If you eat at 3 or at 7, move the day-of bands together so the gaps between tasks survive. Rescheduling one row while the rest stand still is how the oven gets double-booked.
  3. Two dishes at the same temperature ride one rack plan. Write the temp once in the oven box and give them the same slot. Every dish that needs its own temperature is a dish that costs you a turn of the oven you did not budget for.
  4. The main is done when the thermometer says so, not when the clock does. Schedule the rest gap between out-of-oven and carving, and let the schedule absorb a main that runs fifteen minutes long. The clock estimates; the thermometer decides; the rest gap covers the difference.

Pitch deck mistakes: 8 common errors and how to fix them

A free guide to the pitch deck errors that show up most often: eight mistakes, each with one line on why it costs you and one line on how to fix it. Everything here is generic presentation craft, so it applies whether the room is a funding conversation, a client pitch, or an internal review.

Eight common pitch deck mistakes

Each row is one mistake, the reason it hurts, and the fix. None of these depend on what you're selling or who you're pitching.

MistakeWhy it costs youThe fix
1. Opening with the product instead of the problemListeners have no reason to care until they feel the problem; a product-first opening asks them to care on credit.Spend the first slides on the problem, then introduce what you built as the answer to it.
2. Slides written as documentsWalls of text make the room read instead of listen, and people read faster than you talk, so they finish before you and stop paying attention.One idea per slide, and move the detail into a leave-behind you send afterward.
3. No explicit ask"We're looking for support" gives the room nothing to act on; vague asks get vague responses.End with one slide that states the ask as a number or a concrete next step, and say it out loud.
4. Feature dump with no orderTen equal-weight features bury the one that actually solves the problem.Lead with the feature that answers the problem from slide one, and park the rest in an appendix.
5. Numbers with no baselineA big number means nothing on its own; the room silently asks "compared to what?" and moves on if the slide doesn't answer.Pair every number with a baseline or a source, even a rough one you can defend out loud.
6. A roadmap with no dates, or fantasy datesUndated roadmaps can't be checked, so they read as decoration; over-precise dates get checked and missed.Use dates you would defend, with one line on what has to be true for each milestone.
7. A competition slide that claims there is noneEveryone in the room already uses some alternative, including spreadsheets and doing nothing, so the claim costs credibility the moment it lands.Name what people use today and say what your approach changes about it.
8. Reading slides word for wordThe audience reads ahead, so your narration arrives second and adds nothing.Put the point in the slide title and say what the slide doesn't show.

Four opinions

These are opinions from building decks this way, not rules. Disregard any of them once your own rooms say otherwise.

  1. (Opinion.) Fewer slides. Most stories land harder in twelve to fifteen slides than in forty. Cut anything the decision in the room doesn't need, and keep the appendix for questions.
  2. (Opinion.) Outline in plain text before opening slide software. Editors make layout easy before the story holds, which is how documents-on-slides happen. A text outline shows the gaps while they're still cheap to fix.
  3. (Opinion.) One deck per audience. Copy the file and cut for each room. A deck built to serve investors, customers, and recruits at once drifts toward mush because every slide has to work three ways.
  4. (Opinion.) Test the headline read. Read only the slide titles, in order. If the story holds from titles alone, the deck is solid; if it doesn't, restructure before polishing anything.

Self-review: five checks before you present

Run this once the deck feels finished. If a box won't tick, the fix belongs to one of the eight mistakes above.

  • ☐ The ask has a number or a concrete next step, not a vibe.
  • ☐ Each slide makes one point, stated in its title.
  • ☐ Every number on a slide has a baseline or a source you can defend out loud.
  • ☐ Every chart is readable in five seconds without a narrator.
  • ☐ You could cut one more slide and the story would still hold.

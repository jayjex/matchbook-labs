Four opinions on quoting

These are opinions from writing quotes, not survey data and not legal guidance. Take what fits your clients and agreements.

  1. Put a valid-until date on every quote, no exceptions. A quote with no expiry sits open in the client's inbox and in your own head for months, and the price on it can outlive the month you could still do the work for it. Two to four weeks covers most projects; the exact number matters less than having a date on the page.
  2. Write the scope as work you can picture finishing. "Website help" fits anything and everything. "Home page redesign, 5 sections, 2 rounds of feedback" can be checked against reality. When a request falls outside the lines you wrote, it is a new line item, not free extra effort.
  3. Write the payment split as two numbers, not a paragraph. "50% to start, 50% on delivery" is clearer at a glance than any sentence about schedules, and it tells the client the work starts when the first number lands. Keep the same split across clients until a real reason changes it.
  4. Itemize so the client can subtract. One big number gets a yes or a no. Six lines with quantities get a conversation about which lines stay. A client who drops one line item is still a client; a client who swallows one big number and winces may just go quiet.

Quote vs invoice, in three lines

  1. A quote comes before the work. It says what you will deliver and for how much, and it is the page the client signs to agree.
  2. An invoice comes after (or during) the work. It asks for payment against what the quote promised, and it cites the same scope and prices.
  3. They share numbers on purpose. The item lines and total should match between the two, so nobody has to reconcile two different documents at payment time.

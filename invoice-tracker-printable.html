Invoice Tracker Printable

Every invoice you send sits in one of three states, and paper makes all three visible at a glance: sent, paid, overdue. This page gives those states one place to land. Print two pages free: a 12-row invoice log where you write the invoice number, a client alias, the sent date and the amount, then circle where the invoice stands. Page two holds an outstanding recap and a follow-up log. Write-in only, no software needed, prints tonight.

How to use this page: print at 100% scale (site chrome drops out on paper). Page one is the log: one row per invoice, written the day it goes out, number first, then a client alias, then the sent date, then the amount, then circle sent, paid or overdue. When money lands, circle paid. Page two closes the loop: copy the still-unpaid rows into the outstanding recap, count days out, and log each follow-up as you send it. Print a fresh pair for each month.

Page one: the invoice log, 12 rows

Twelve rows covers a normal invoicing month for a one-person shop, and a page you can finish beats a ledger you abandon. Nothing is pre-filled: no sample invoices, no amounts someone else made up. The status circles are sorting labels, the same three states every invoice passes through. Circle one as you write the row, and re-circle when the state changes: sent becomes paid, or sent becomes overdue when the due date passes without money. The client column takes an alias, because a sheet of client names is paper you have to guard, and initials identify an invoice just as well on your own desk.

Page two: outstanding recap and follow-up log

The recap is the unpaid side of page one, pulled onto five rows. Copy each invoice still missing its paid circle: number, alias, amount, and days out, counted from the sent date. Five rows is a working shortlist; a log with more than five unpaid lines is telling you something worth reading before you send anything new. Below it sits the follow-up log, six rows of date, who, and what was said. One line per chase. A dated history of reminders does more for the next conversation than memory does, and it stops the double-email a day apart.

Four opinions on running the log

Opinions from building record-keeping pages like this one. Take or leave:

  1. (Opinion.) Number invoices sequentially and never reuse a number. A gap in the sequence is information: it tells you whether an invoice was retired, voided or never sent. Made-up or reused numbers scramble that signal, and they look off to a client's bookkeeper too.
  2. (Opinion.) Write the row the same day the invoice goes out. The sent date drives everything downstream: the recap, the days-out count, the follow-up timing. Reconstructing it later means digging through sent mail for a date you could have written in four seconds.
  3. (Opinion.) Use the client alias, keep the real name where the invoice itself lives. The alias column exists because paper travels: it gets photographed, left on a desk, binned loose. Initials or a first-name-plus-initial identify the row for you, and the full name sits in the signed invoice and the contract anyway.
  4. (Opinion.) Chase from the recap, not from memory, and log every chase. Pick one day a week, work down the five recap rows, and write one line per follow-up in the log. "I should email them again" becomes a dated history, and the next reminder can reference the last one instead of starting over.

Garage Sale Checklist Printable

A garage sale is four deadlines stacked on top of each other: two weeks out, one week out, the day before, and the morning of. Miss one and the rest gets rushed. This page has the whole run-up on one printable sheet: 20 checkboxes split across those four checkpoints, an 8-row pricing sheet where every asking price stays a write-in (you know your stuff, a template doesn't), a change-box line for the float you start the day with, four signage opinions, a plan for the leftovers, and a weather line. The cleaning checklist covers a different kind of sale prep; this one is for the weekend your garage becomes a store. Everything here is free and prints on one page.

How to use this page: print at 100% scale (the site chrome drops out on paper). Write the sale date at the top, then work the four boxes in order and tick items off as they're done. When a price gets stickered, copy it onto the pricing sheet so the day-of math happens on paper, not in your head. Fill the change-box and leftovers lines before sale morning, because both are decisions that go badly at 7am.

The sheet: 20 checkboxes and the day's numbers

The timeline runs top to bottom in two columns, newest deadline never outranking the box above it. The pricing rows start with common categories, but any row can be renamed; the asking column stays blank on purpose. Nobody else can look at your tools and tell you what they're worth to you.

Garage sale prep checklist

Sale date:   Hours:

2 weeks out

  • Pick the date, the hours, and a rain decision
  • Walk every room, list what actually goes
  • Set a "sale" box in each closet
  • Ask family + neighbors to add their stuff
  • Count your tables, chairs, extension cords

1 week out

  • Sticker every item with its asking price
  • Copy the prices onto the sheet below
  • Post the listing with photos of the good stuff
  • Line up one helper for the day
  • Gather bags, boxes, newspaper, marker

Day before

  • Make signs: 3 words, huge address number
  • Set up tables, group items by category
  • Plug in + test anything electric
  • Count the change float, write it below
  • Charge phone, stash a pen and tape

Morning of

  • Put signs out, arrows at every turn
  • Eye-catching items near the street
  • Cash box out of sight but reachable
  • Walkway clear, open on time
  • Log what sold as it sells

Four opinions on signs

Signs are the whole ad budget for a garage sale, so how they're made matters more than where they're stored. These are opinions from watching which signs actually pulled cars, take or leave:

  1. Three words, huge letters. "GARAGE SALE" and the address number, and that's it. A sign someone reads from a moving car works; a sign with the full itinerary doesn't.
  2. An arrow at every turn. One sign at the main road and a left turn nobody announced loses most of the cars you paid for in attention. Put an arrow wherever the route could go two ways.
  3. The address number is the biggest thing on the sign. People navigate by the number once they're on your street, and it's the part they're trying to read while slowing down.
  4. Take them down the same evening. A sale sign still up on Tuesday quietly burns whatever patience the neighborhood has left for the next one. Write the takedown into the morning-of box's opposite: the close.

Why the float goes on paper

The change box is the only part of a garage sale with real money math, and it's the part people improvise. Write the starting float on the sheet the night before and count the box to match it. Then "how the day went" is a clean subtraction at close, and any gap has exactly two places to look. Whatever float you pick, the number on paper is what makes it countable; there's no right amount on this sheet, only your amount.

What's next

The timeline covers the run-up; the pricing sheet and float line cover the day itself. The leftovers box is the part that keeps the whole thing from being pointless: whatever doesn't sell needs a named destination before the morning starts, whether that's a shelf, a donation drop-off, or the curb. If the sale went well enough to be a habit, the next one needs this same sheet again, which is why it prints free.
